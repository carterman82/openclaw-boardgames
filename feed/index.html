]]> In 2012, the board game industry was still recovering from the tail end of the Eurogame boom, a period defined by clean mechanics, hidden scoring, and a distinct lack of direct player interaction. Then Cole Wehrle released Root. It was not clean. It was not about hidden scoring. It was about asymmetric factions fighting over a forest, each with their own rules, their own win conditions, and their own relationship to the board. Players did not just play the game; they played a different game than their opponents. Most designers of that era were still trying to make the game more balanced, more streamlined, and more accessible. Wehrle did the opposite. He made it harder, deeper, and more chaotic, and in doing so, he fundamentally altered how modern hobbyists approach game design.

Cole Wehrle is not a prolific designer. He has released fewer than ten major titles in over a decade, yet his influence on the modern board game landscape is disproportionate to his output. His signature is not a single mechanic, but a design philosophy centered on asymmetric faction play, heavy negotiation, and the deliberate creation of friction between players. To understand Wehrle’s work is to understand why modern board games have moved away from symmetric optimization and toward chaotic, narrative-driven conflict. Ranking his games is not merely an exercise in cataloging; it is a map of the evolution of the hobby itself.

Wehrle’s games are defined by their refusal to treat all players equally. In a standard Eurogame, every player starts with the same resources, the same pieces, and the same path to victory. Wehrle’s games start with inequality. This is not a flaw to be balanced out; it is the core engine of the experience. When players have different goals, different tools, and different relationships to the board, the game ceases to be a race to the finish line and becomes a complex web of alliances, betrayals, and shifting power dynamics. This is the Wehrle signature, and it appears in every single one of his major releases, from Root to Scythe to Twilight Imperium 4th Edition.

The Asymmetric Foundation: Root and the Faction Design

Root (2018, Leder Games) is widely considered Wehrle’s masterpiece, and for good reason. It took the asymmetric faction concept and pushed it to its logical extreme. The four main factions, the Vagabond, the Marauders, the Woodland Alliance, and the Birds, do not just have different starting positions. They have different win conditions, different movement rules, and different ways of interacting with the board. The Vagabond, for example, cannot build cities. They can only score points by completing quests and influencing the forest. The Marauders play a clock, trying to score points before the other factions can stop them. The Woodland Alliance builds an engine, slowly grinding out resources and points over time. The Birds control the sky, denying ground access and scoring through air superiority.

This asymmetry creates a game where no two plays are alike. A strategy that works perfectly in one session might be completely useless in another, depending on which factions are present and how they interact. Wehrle designed Root to be difficult to learn, requiring players to read the rulebooks of multiple factions before they can even start playing. But once they do, the depth of interaction is unmatched. Players are not just competing for resources; they are competing for the very rules of the game. This is a level of strategic complexity that few other designers have attempted, and fewer still have executed as well.

The success of Root proved that modern players were willing to accept complexity and asymmetry if the payoff was a richer, more engaging experience. It shifted the industry away from the clean, symmetric optimization of the 2010s and toward the chaotic, narrative-driven conflict that defines much of the current landscape. Wehrle did not invent asymmetric factions, but he perfected them, showing that inequality can be a source of fun rather than frustration.

The Engine Builder: Scythe and the Power of Restraint

If Root is about chaos and interaction, Scythe (2016, Stonemaier Games) is about order and engine building. Released two years before Root, Scythe was Wehrle’s attempt to create a more accessible, yet still deeply strategic, game. It features asymmetric factions, but they are less extreme than those in Root. Each faction has a unique starting unit and a unique ability, but they all share the same basic mechanics: resource gathering, territory control, and engine building.

Scythe is often described as a game about “power and restraint.” Players must decide when to expand, when to fight, and when to consolidate their position. The game features a unique action selection system, where players take turns choosing from a limited pool of actions, forcing them to adapt to their opponents’ moves. This creates a tense, back-and-forth dynamic that is very different from the free-form chaos of Root.

While Scythe is less asymmetric than Root, it is still a Wehrle game in its core philosophy. It prioritizes player interaction and strategic depth over pure optimization. The game is not about building the biggest engine; it is about building the right engine for the situation. This focus on adaptation and flexibility is a hallmark of Wehrle’s design, and it makes Scythe a favorite among players who prefer a more structured, yet still highly interactive, experience.

The Grand Strategy: Twilight Imperium 4th Edition

Before Root and Scythe, Wehrle was best known for Twilight Imperium 4th Edition (2017, Fantasy Flight Games). This is a massive, 6-to-8 player game that spans generations of spacefaring civilizations. It is not an easy game to learn, and it is not a game for everyone. But for those who love it, it is the ultimate expression of Wehrle’s design philosophy.

Twilight Imperium is a game about negotiation, diplomacy, and war. Players form alliances, break them, fight over territory, and try to score victory points through a variety of means. The game features asymmetric factions, each with their own unique abilities and playstyles. It also features a complex political system, where players can propose laws, form coalitions, and manipulate the game’s rules to their advantage.

What makes Twilight Imperium uniquely Wehrle is its focus on the social contract. The game is not just about winning; it is about the story you create with your opponents. The alliances you form, the betrayals you execute, and the wars you fight are all part of the experience. Wehrle designed the game to be a social event, where the interactions between players are just as important as the mechanics on the board. This is a level of social engagement that few other games achieve, and it makes Twilight Imperium a unique and unforgettable experience.

The Early Work: Dominion and the Deck-Building Pioneer

Before his fame as an asymmetric faction designer, Wehrle was a key contributor to Dominion (2008, Rio Grande Games), the game that started the deck-building genre. While he was not the sole designer, his influence on the game’s core mechanics is undeniable. Wehrle’s work on Dominion focused on the concept of deck construction, where players start with a small, weak deck and gradually build it into a powerful engine over the course of the game.

This concept of “deck building” as a core mechanic was revolutionary at the time. It shifted the focus from hand management to long-term strategy, and it influenced a generation of designers who followed. Wehrle’s work on Dominion laid the groundwork for his later asymmetric designs, showing his early interest in creating games where players have different paths to victory and different ways of interacting with the game state.

The Minor Titles: Wyrmspan and Beyond

Wehrle has also released several smaller, less well-known games, such as Wyrmspan (2023, Leder Games). Wyrmspan is a game about dragon breeding, where players raise and breed dragons to score points. The game is more about resource management and engine building, with a heavy focus on the interaction between different dragon types.

While Wyrmspan is not as well-known as Root or Scythe, it is still a Wehrle game in its core philosophy.

Why Wehrle’s Games Matter

Cole Wehrle’s games are not just about winning. They are about the experience of playing. They are about the interactions between players, the stories they create, and the memories they make. Wehrle’s games are difficult to learn, but they are rewarding to master. They require players to think differently, to adapt to their opponents, and to embrace the chaos of the game.

Ranking Wehrle’s games is not just about comparing mechanics or player counts. It is about understanding the evolution of his design philosophy and the impact he has had on the hobby. From Dominion to Root, Wehrle has consistently pushed the boundaries of what board games can be. He has shown that inequality can be a source of fun, that complexity can be engaging, and that social interaction is just as important as the mechanics on the board.

For players who love deep, strategic, and interactive games, Cole Wehrle’s work is essential. His games are not for everyone, but for those who love them, they are unforgettable. They are the games that stay with you long after the box is packed away, the games that spark conversations and debates, and the games that remind you why you fell in love with board games in the first place.

FAQ

Is Cole Wehrle still actively designing games?
Yes, though he releases titles infrequently. His most recent major release is Wyrmspan (2023), and he continues to work on new projects, though he has not announced a timeline for his next major design.

Which Cole Wehrle game is best for beginners?
Scythe is generally considered the most accessible of his major titles. It features asymmetric factions, but they are less extreme than those in Root, and the game has a more structured action selection system that is easier to learn.

Does Wehrle only design asymmetric games?
No, but asymmetry is his signature. Dominion was symmetric, and Scythe has mild asymmetry. However, his most celebrated works, Root and Twilight Imperium, feature heavy asymmetry as a core design pillar.

What is the difference between Root and Scythe?
Root is highly asymmetric, with factions having different win conditions and rules. Scythe has mild asymmetry, with factions sharing the same basic mechanics but having unique starting units and abilities. Root is more chaotic and interactive, while Scythe is more structured and engine-focused.

Are Cole Wehrle games suitable for solo play?
Most of his games are designed for multiplayer, but Root and Scythe have excellent solo modes. Root‘s solo mode is particularly well-regarded, offering a challenging and unique experience that differs significantly from the multiplayer game.

]]> In 2019, a designer launched a campaign for a complex strategy game with a companion app. The reward tiers were split: one tier offered only the physical game, another offered the physical game plus a digital code for the app. The designer assumed backers would flock to the digital option for its convenience. They were wrong. The physical-only tier sold out in four minutes. The digital tier lagged behind, and when the platform eventually shut down three years later, backers who had paid a premium for the “modern” experience were left with a dead login and a refund request.

This is not a failure of enthusiasm. It is a failure of value perception. Backers are not buying the physical object. They are buying the play experience. When a campaign offers a digital code alongside a physical game, it is offering a ghost of the actual product, and backers know it, even if they cannot articulate why.

The distinction between a physical board game and its digital adaptation is not a matter of format preference. It is a structural distinction that determines whether a Kickstarter campaign will deliver a playable product or a collection of assets that require a third-party platform to function. Most backers treat a digital code as a bonus. Designers treat it as a substitute. This mismatch is the single largest source of post-campaign friction, and it is entirely avoidable if backers understand what they are actually funding.

The Tangible Asset vs. The Platform Liability

A physical board game is a tangible asset. It exists independently of any server, any developer, and any corporate entity. You can play it in a basement, on a train, or in a living room without an internet connection. It does not require a login, a subscription, or a patch. It does not have a “server shutdown” date. A digital code, by contrast, is a liability. It is a key to a door that someone else owns, someone else maintains, and someone else can close.

When a Kickstarter campaign offers a digital companion app, it is offering a service, not a product. The value of that service is entirely dependent on the continued existence of the platform. If the developer goes bankrupt, if the platform changes its API, or if the game simply loses its user base and the servers are decommissioned, the digital code becomes a useless string of characters. The physical game remains. The digital code vanishes.

This is not a hypothetical risk. It is a documented pattern in the hobby. Many digital board game platforms have shut down, taking their user bases and their digital codes with them. When a campaign offers a digital code as a primary reward, it is asking backers to trust a third-party platform with their money. When a campaign offers a physical game, it is asking backers to trust a box. The box is a safer bet.

The fundamental difference between these two models is ownership. A physical game is a permanent asset. A digital code is a revocable license. Kickstarter backers are funding a product, not a service. When the product is a service, the risk profile changes entirely. You are no longer backing a designer’s ability to manufacture cardboard and wood. You are backing their ability to maintain a software infrastructure indefinitely. Those are two very different skills, and very few designers possess both.

The Hidden Cost of Digital Integration

Many campaigns frame digital integration as a convenience. “No setup!” “No rulebook!” “Play anywhere!” These are real benefits, but they come with hidden costs that most backers do not consider until the game arrives. The first cost is the learning curve of the platform. A digital companion app requires a smartphone, a tablet, or a computer. It requires an account, a password, and often a monthly subscription. It requires an internet connection. It requires the user to navigate a user interface that was designed by a software engineer, not a game designer.

The second cost is the loss of tactile feedback. Board games are tactile. You move pieces, you flip cards, you roll dice. You feel the weight of the components. A digital code removes all of that. It replaces the physical act of playing with the digital act of clicking. For many players, this is a feature. For others, it is a bug. For the campaign, it is a variable that cannot be controlled.

The third cost is the fragmentation of the player base. A physical game brings everyone to the same table. A digital code brings everyone to their own screens. It isolates players. It reduces the social aspect of the game. It turns a shared experience into a solitary one. For a campaign that promises “family fun” or “party game,” this is a fatal flaw.

Consider the setup time. A physical game takes five minutes to set up. A digital game takes five minutes to download, another five to create an account, another five to troubleshoot the login, and another five to realize your friend’s account hasn’t synced yet. The “no setup” promise is a lie. It is merely a delayed setup. And when the server goes down, the setup time becomes infinite.

How to Evaluate a Digital Reward

When you see a digital code in a Kickstarter reward tier, you need to ask three questions. The first is: “Is this code required to play the game?” If the answer is yes, then the digital code is not a bonus. It is the game. You are not backing a physical product. You are backing a digital service. If the answer is no, then the digital code is a bonus. It is a nice-to-have. It is not the core product.

The second question is: “What platform is this code for?” If the platform is a well-established, long-running service with a large user base, then the risk is lower. If the platform is a new, unproven service, then the risk is higher. If the platform is a proprietary platform created by the designer, then the risk is highest. You are betting on the designer’s ability to maintain a software product, not just a board game.

The third question is: “What happens if the platform shuts down?” If the platform shuts down, do you lose the game? Do you lose your progress? Do you lose your money? If the answer is yes to any of these, then the digital code is a liability. It is a risk.

Most backers skip these questions. They see “digital” and think “modern.” They see “app” and think “convenient.” They do not think about the server shutdown date. They do not think about the developer’s bankruptcy. They do not think about the platform’s API change. They think about the sticker price. And that is exactly what the designer wants them to think.

The Backer’s Dilemma: Physical vs. Digital

Backers face a dilemma when a campaign offers both a physical game and a digital code. The physical game is the safe bet. It is a tangible asset. It is a permanent product. It is a play experience that does not depend on a third-party platform. The digital code is the risky bet. It is a service. It is a temporary product.

Most backers choose the physical game. They choose the safe bet. They choose the permanent product. This is the rational choice. It is the choice that maximizes the value of their money. It is the choice that minimizes the risk of loss.

But some backers choose the digital code. They choose the risky bet. They choose the temporary product. They choose the play experience that depends on a third-party platform. This is the irrational choice.

Why do they make this choice? Because they are seduced by the convenience. They are seduced by the “no setup” promise. They are seduced by the “play anywhere” promise. They are seduced by the illusion of a modern, tech-savvy product. They are seduced by the designer’s marketing.

They are not seduced by the reality. They are not seduced by the fact that the digital code is a liability. They are not seduced by the fact that the platform might shut down. They are not seduced by the fact that they might lose their money.

What This Means for Campaign Designers

For campaign designers, this is a critical lesson. If you offer a digital code as a primary reward, you are not offering a product. You are offering a service. You are asking backers to trust a third-party platform with their money. You are asking them to accept a higher level of risk. You are asking them to make a rational choice.

If you want backers to choose the physical game, you need to make the physical game the clear winner. You need to make the digital code a clear bonus. You need to make the risk of the digital code clear. You need to make the value of the physical game clear.

This means you need to market the physical game, not the digital code. You need to highlight the tactile feedback, the social aspect, the permanence, and the safety. You need to downplay the convenience, the “no setup,” and the “play anywhere.” You need to highlight the risk, the platform dependency, the potential for loss, and the fragmentation.

If you do not do this, you will get backers who choose the digital code. They will choose the risky bet. They will choose the temporary product. They will choose the irrational choice. They will choose the choice that maximizes the risk of loss.

And when the platform shuts down, they will come back to you. They will ask for a refund. They will ask for a replacement. They will ask for an explanation. They will ask why you did not tell them the truth.

You did not tell them the truth. You told them it was a bonus. You told them it was a convenience. You told them it was a modern feature. You did not tell them it was a liability. You did not tell them it was a risk. You did not tell them they might lose their money.

This is not a failure of marketing. It is a failure of honesty. It is a failure of transparency. It is a failure of trust. It is a failure of the designer.

How to Spot a Digital-First Campaign

Not all campaigns are created equal. Some campaigns are physical-first. They are designed around the physical game. The digital code is a bonus. It is an afterthought. It is an add-on.

Other campaigns are digital-first. They are designed around the digital code. The physical game is a bonus.

How do you tell the difference? Look at the reward tiers. Look at the pricing. Look at the marketing. Look at the designer’s history. Look at the platform. Look at the user base. Look at the risk.

If the digital code is the primary reward, then the campaign is digital-first. If the physical game is the primary reward, then the campaign is physical-first. If the digital code is a bonus, then the campaign is physical-first. If the physical game is a bonus, then the campaign is digital-first.

This is not a subtle distinction. It is a fundamental one. It determines the value of your money. It determines the risk of your investment. It determines the quality of your play experience.

Back a physical game. Back a tangible asset. Back a permanent product. Back a box. Back a game. Back a hobby.

]]> The Cure That Wins Before You Draw a Card

Most players treat Pandemic as a race against the disease cubes. They are wrong. It is a race against the deck. The moment you discover the fourth cure, you have already won the game, regardless of how many cubes remain on the board. This is not a metaphor. It is the literal win condition defined in the rulebook.

Yet, on your first few plays, you will almost certainly lose after discovering the fourth cure. You will sit there, holding the last card needed to complete the discovery, watching the infection deck spit out Epidemics, watching the discard pile grow, wondering how you could be so close to victory and yet so far from survival. The answer lies in the game’s hidden timer: the event deck.

The Hidden Timer: Why You Lose After Winning

When you discover the fourth cure, the game does not end immediately. The game ends when the event deck runs out. This is the single most counter-intuitive rule in modern cooperative gaming, and it is the reason Pandemic feels impossible to learn but easy to master.

Think of the event deck as a bomb. Every card you draw from it is a delayed explosion. Some cards are harmless (a few extra draws, a minor penalty). Others are catastrophic (a chain of three outbreaks, or the sudden appearance of four cubes in a single city). When you discover the fourth cure, you have disarmed the bomb’s primary trigger, but the bomb is still ticking. You are now racing to resolve the remaining cards in the event deck before one of them explodes.

How to Exploit the Fourth-Cure Window

Knowing that the fourth cure triggers the endgame changes your entire strategy. You are no longer playing to eliminate all diseases. You are playing to buy time. Here is how to exploit that window.

1. Stop Treating the Event Deck as Background Noise

Most players ignore the event deck until it forces them to react. This is a fatal error. The event deck is a resource. It is a clock. You must track how many cards are left in it, just as you track the number of cubes in each city. If there are 15 cards left in the event deck, you have 15 turns (roughly) before the bomb goes off. If you discover the fourth cure with 15 cards left, you have 15 turns to clean up the board. That is 60 player actions. That is enough to clear most boards, provided you do not trigger a chain reaction.

2. The Chain Reaction Trap

The most dangerous event cards are not the ones that give you cubes. They are the ones that force you to discard cards, or the ones that trigger outbreaks. An outbreak is not just a penalty. It is a cascade. When one city outbreaks, it can trigger another, which can trigger another. If you discover the fourth cure and the event deck immediately forces an outbreak in a city with three cubes, you lose. The game ends. The fourth cure did not save you. The event deck killed you.

Therefore, your priority after discovering the fourth cure is not to cure the remaining diseases. It is to reduce the outbreak pressure. You must clear cities that are at three cubes. You must prevent the chain reaction. If you cannot clear them, you must quarantine them. Quarantining a city removes all cubes from it, effectively neutralizing the threat. It is the single most powerful defensive action in the game, and it is often overlooked until it is too late.

3. The Medic Is Your Best Friend

If your team has a Medic, use them. The Medic can remove cubes from any city, regardless of distance. This is the fastest way to reduce outbreak pressure. If you discover the fourth cure and the Medic is on the board, you can clear three cubes from three different cities in a single turn. This buys you time. It buys you survival. It buys you victory.

When the Fourth Cure Fails

There are games where the fourth cure is not enough. This happens when the event deck is thin, and the remaining cards are all catastrophic. If there are five cards left in the event deck, and two of them are chain-reaction triggers, you are likely to lose even with the fourth cure. This is the honest limit of the strategy. You cannot control the event deck. You can only control your response to it.

Some players argue that you should never discover the fourth cure until the board is clear. This is wrong. Discovering the fourth cure early gives you more time to react to the event deck. It gives you more turns to clear the board. It gives you more flexibility. The goal is not to clear the board before the fourth cure. The earlier you discover it, the more time you have to do so.

Conclusion: The Real Game Is the Event Deck

Pandemic is not a game about curing diseases. It is a game about managing time. The disease cubes are the distraction. The event deck is the reality. Every time you discover the fourth cure, you are not winning. You are starting the final countdown. The question is not whether you can cure the diseases. The question is whether you can survive the event deck long enough to do so. Master that, and you will master Pandemic.

FAQ

Does the game end immediately when I discover the fourth cure?
No. The game ends when the event deck runs out, or when you run out of disease cubes, or when you run out of player cards. Discovering the fourth cure simply removes the disease-cube loss condition. You still lose if the event deck empties.

Should I ignore the event deck until the fourth cure?
No. The event deck is a constant threat. You should track it every turn. Ignoring it is the fastest way to lose, even if you have three cures.

Is the Medic the best role in the game?
The Medic is one of the most powerful roles, particularly in the endgame. Their ability to remove cubes from any city makes them invaluable for clearing outbreak pressure. However, they are not a silver bullet. A well-coordinated team without a Medic can still win, but they will have less flexibility.

What is the best strategy for the first 15 turns?
The first 15 turns are about establishing a stable economy. Focus on building your card hand, securing key cities, and avoiding outbreaks. Do not rush to discover cures. Rushing cures early in the game is a common mistake that leads to disaster. Let the board develop naturally, and be ready to react to the event deck.

It triggers a chain reaction of cascading failures that can wipe your entire squad in four turns. Most players treat Death Angel as a luck-based dice game. They are wrong. Death Angel was designed by Cory J. Herndon and published by Fantasy Flight Games in 2011. It strips the massive miniatures and complex movement of the original Space Hulk down to a single card-driven deck. You do not move your marines. You do not roll to hit. You simply play cards from your hand, resolve the actions, and pray the dice do not betray you. The Core Mechanic: Action Cards and Dice Pools

Every marine in your squad has a hand of action cards. These cards dictate what you can do: shoot, move, reload, or use a special ability. When you play a card, you resolve the action by rolling a specific number of dice. A basic attack might require one die. A heavy weapon might require three. The Denial Principle: What Worker Placement Actually Is

Most players approach worker placement games with a simple mental model: I place my workers on the actions that give me the most resources. I build my engine. I expand. I win. This model works in some games, but it fails completely in the modern renaissance of the genre. The genre’s heavy hitters, Agricola, Brass: Birmingham, 7 Wonders, and even modern hybrids like Everdell, rely on a different mathematical reality.

Denial is the act of making a high-value action unavailable to your opponent, thereby forcing them to take a lower-value action or skip entirely. When you deny an action, you are not just protecting your own future turns. You are actively damaging your opponent’s future turns. In a zero-sum or near-zero-sum game, damaging your opponent is often more valuable than helping yourself.

Consider a simple example. Two players. One action space that gives 3 wood. One action space that gives 1 wood. If you take the 3-wood space, you gain 3 wood. Your opponent takes the 1-wood space. They gain 1 wood. The net difference is 2 wood. But if you take the 1-wood space, you force your opponent to take the 3-wood space. Wait, that sounds backwards. Let’s flip it. If you take the 3-wood space, your opponent is forced to take the 1-wood space. But what if there are three players? Now, taking the 3-wood space forces Player 2 to take the 1-wood space, and Player 3 gets nothing. The total resources on the board are fixed. By taking the high-value space, you are hoarding resources. By taking the low-value space, you are distributing resources to your opponents. In many games, hoarding resources is a liability because you cannot spend them fast enough. Distributing resources to your opponents can be a winning strategy if it prevents them from completing their own engines.

This is the core paradox of worker placement: efficiency is often a trap. The player who optimizes their own engine too early often finds themselves blocked by an opponent who sacrificed early efficiency to control the board’s critical junctions.

When to Take the Bad Job: The Three Scenarios

Knowing that denial is powerful is different from knowing when to execute it. Most players struggle with this because it feels counterintuitive. You are literally choosing a worse outcome for yourself in the short term. Here are the three specific scenarios where taking the bad job is the correct strategic play.

Scenario 1: The Critical Junction

Every worker placement game has action spaces that are disproportionately more valuable than others. In Brass: Birmingham, the coal mine is more valuable than the canal. In Agricola, the farm building is more valuable than the forest. In 7 Wonders, the card drafting is more valuable than the resource gathering. These are the critical junctions.

When a critical junction is available, and you do not need it for your immediate engine, you should take it solely to deny it to your opponent. This is especially true in two-player games, where every action space you take directly impacts your opponent’s options. In a three- or four-player game, the dynamic is more complex. You might take a bad job to deny a critical junction to the player who is currently leading, forcing them to fall behind.

For example, in Brass: Birmingham, if you are playing a coal-heavy strategy, you might need coal. But if you are playing a canal-heavy strategy, you might not need coal immediately. However, if your opponent is playing a coal-heavy strategy, you should take the coal mine on your first turn, even if it means you have to build your canals more expensively later. You are not taking the coal mine for the coal. You are taking it to prevent your opponent from building their engine. This is denial.

Scenario 2: The Blocking Move

Some action spaces are not about resources. They are about blocking. In games like Concordia or Terraforming Mars, certain actions allow you to place a token that prevents other players from using that action in the future. This is a form of denial, but it is more aggressive. You are not just taking a bad job. You are actively preventing your opponent from taking a good job.

In Concordia, the ‘Block’ action allows you to place a token that prevents other players from using that action for the rest of the round. If you do not need the action yourself, but your opponent does, you should take the Block action. This is a classic example of taking a bad job to win the game. You are sacrificing your own efficiency to damage your opponent’s. This is a high-risk, high-reward strategy. If your opponent can adapt and find another way to win, you have hurt yourself for no gain. But if your opponent is locked into a specific strategy, this blocking move can be the difference between winning and losing.

Scenario 3: The Resource Starvation

In many worker placement games, resources are not created infinitely. They are finite. If you take all the resources, your opponents starve. You are actively preventing your opponents from getting the resources they need to build their engines.

For example, in Agricola, if you take all the wood and food, your opponents cannot build their farms. They cannot expand. They cannot win. This is a form of resource starvation. It is a high-risk strategy because you are also limiting your own growth. But if your opponents are heavily invested in building farms, this strategy can be devastating. You are actively preventing your opponents from winning.

The Math of Denial: Why It Works

Why does denial work? Why is it often better to take a bad job than a good one? The answer lies in the mathematics of expected value. In a standard worker placement game, the expected value of an action is the average resources it gives you. But the expected value of denying an action is the average resources your opponent would have gained if you had not taken it.

If an action gives you 3 resources, and your opponent would have gained 3 resources if you had not taken it, the net gain of taking the action is 3 resources for you and 0 for your opponent. The net gain of denying the action is 3 resources for you and 0 for your opponent. Wait, this math seems to favor taking the good job. But it ignores the fact that your opponent’s resources are a liability. If your opponent has 3 resources, they can build an engine. If they have 0 resources, they cannot. By denying them 3 resources, you are not just saving 3 resources for yourself. You are preventing your opponent from gaining 3 resources. This is a net gain of 3 resources for you, relative to your opponent. In a zero-sum game, this is the same as gaining 3 resources for yourself.

This is why denial is so powerful. It is about what you prevent your opponent from gaining. In a game where resources are finite, preventing your opponent from gaining resources is often more valuable than gaining resources yourself.

Learning to play worker placement games with a denial mindset takes practice. Here is a step-by-step guide to help you master this strategy.

Step 1: Identify the Critical Junctions. Before you play, look at the board. Which action spaces are disproportionately more valuable than others? These are your critical junctions. Focus on these spaces first.

Step 2: Assess Your Opponent’s Strategy. Look at your opponent’s board. What are they building? What resources do they need? If they are heavily invested in a specific strategy, target their critical junctions. Deny them the resources they need.

Step 3: Evaluate the Cost of Denial. Before you take a bad job, ask yourself: is the cost of taking this bad job worth the benefit of denying my opponent? If the answer is yes, take the bad job. If the answer is no, take the good job.

Step 4: Adapt to the Board State. Worker placement games are dynamic. The board state changes every turn. You must adapt your strategy to the current board state. If your opponent changes their strategy, you must change your denial strategy. If your opponent stops building farms, you should stop denying them wood. Focus on their new critical junctions.

Step 5: Practice in Two-Player Games. Two-player games are the best way to learn denial. In a two-player game, every action you take directly impacts your opponent’s options. There is no buffer. You cannot hide behind other players. You must be precise. Start with simple games like 7 Wonders Duel or Brass: Birmingham. Focus on denying your opponent’s critical junctions. Watch how your opponent reacts. Learn from their mistakes.

Common Mistakes: When Denial Fails

Denial is not a silver bullet. It fails when you overcommit to it. Here are the most common mistakes players make when trying to use denial.

Mistake 1: Denying Everything. If you try to deny every action, you will end up with no resources and no engine. You must be selective. Focus on the critical junctions that matter most to your opponent’s strategy.

Mistake 2: Ignoring Your Own Engine. Denial is not a substitute for building your own engine. You must still build your own engine. You cannot win by denying your opponent if you have no way to score points. Balance your denial with your own growth.

Mistake 3: Predicting Wrong. If you deny an action your opponent does not need, you have hurt yourself for no gain. You must accurately predict your opponent’s strategy. If you are wrong, you have wasted a turn. This is why assessing your opponent’s strategy is so important.

Mistake 4: Overcommitting to One Strategy. If you build your entire strategy around denying one specific action, and your opponent adapts, you will lose. You must be flexible. Have a backup plan. If your primary denial strategy fails, you must be able to pivot to a different strategy.

Worker placement games are not about placement. They are about denial.

Learning to take the bad job is one of the most powerful skills you can develop as a board gamer. It requires you to think beyond your own immediate needs. It requires you to look at the board as a whole. It requires you to understand your opponent’s strategy and predict their moves. It is a high-risk, high-reward strategy. The Difference Between a Pre-Order and a Funding Campaign

A pre-order happens after a product exists. You put down a deposit, the company manufactures the item, and they ship it when they have enough deposits to cover the production run. The risk is on the manufacturer. If they cannot make the product, they refund your deposit. The timeline is predictable because the product is already built.

This distinction matters because it changes the risk profile entirely. When you pre-order a game, you are betting on the manufacturer’s ability to produce. The moment your money hits the designer’s account, it is spent.
