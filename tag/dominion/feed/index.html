This article explains the structural differences between deck construction and deck modification, and why treating Ascension like Dominion leads to consistent losses.

]]> You just finished your first game of Dominion. You look at your deck, which now contains a mix of Copper, Estate, and a few Gold cards, and you feel a sense of accomplishment. You have built an engine. You have bought better cards. You have optimized your turns. Then a friend slides a box of Ascension across the table and says, “Try this. It’s like Dominion, but faster.” You open the box, shuffle the cards, and deal out the starting decks. On your very first turn, you buy a card. On your second turn, you buy another. By the fifth turn, you are playing with a deck that looks nothing like the one you started with. You realize something is different, but you cannot quite put your finger on it. It feels like Dominion, but it is not. It is something else entirely, and understanding what that something else is will change how you approach every deck-building game you play from here on out.

Most players treat Ascension and Dominion as interchangeable members of the same family. They are not. They are two distinct approaches to the same design problem: how do you make a player feel powerful without letting them break the game? Dominion solves this problem through deck construction over time. Ascension solves it through deck modification during play. The difference is not cosmetic. It is structural. It changes the rhythm of the game, the nature of the decisions, and the reason you win or lose. If you approach Ascension with Dominion strategies, you will play it wrong. You will hoard when you should spend. You will optimize when you should adapt. You will lose games you should have won because you were playing a game of accumulation in a game of evolution.

The Core Mechanic: Construction vs. Modification

Dominion is a game about building a deck from scratch. You start with a small, weak deck consisting of ten Copper cards and five Estate cards. Your goal is to buy better cards from a shared market, add them to your deck, and hope that your next draw cycle includes enough of those new cards to make your turn powerful. This is a game of construction. You are laying bricks, one by one, waiting for the mortar to set. The power comes from the synergy of the cards you have chosen to include. The risk comes from the cards you have chosen to exclude.

Ascension is a game about modifying a deck that is already in play. You start with a deck that contains ten Attack cards and five Curse cards. Your goal is to replace those weak cards with better ones, but you do not add the new cards to your deck. You remove the old cards from your deck and put the new cards into your discard pile. On your next draw, you will draw the new card instead of the old one. This is a game of modification. You are remodeling a house while you are still living in it. The power comes from the speed of the swap. The risk comes from the timing of the discard.

This distinction is critical. In Dominion, every card you buy stays in your deck until you choose to sell it or discard it through a specific card effect. In Ascension, every card you buy replaces a card in your deck immediately. You are not building a deck. You are curating a hand. This changes the fundamental math of the game. In Dominion, you are managing the size of your deck. In Ascension, you are managing the quality of your deck. One is a game of volume. The other is a game of velocity.

The Tempo Problem: Why Dominion Players Lose at Ascension

When Dominion players first sit down to play Ascension, they make a specific mistake. They treat the Attack and Curse cards as resources to be managed, not as liabilities to be eliminated. In Dominion, Copper and Estate are the baseline. You accept them as the cost of doing business. You play them every turn, and you learn to live with them. They are the floor. In Ascension, Attack and Curse are the ceiling. They are the problem. You do not accept them. You eliminate them as quickly as possible.

The mistake is to hoard. A Dominion player sees a powerful card in the shop and thinks, “I will buy this when I have enough money. I will keep my deck small. I will wait for the perfect moment.” In Ascension, that moment never comes. The game is too fast. The shop refreshes every turn. The power curve is too steep. If you wait three turns to buy a card, you have already lost those three turns. You have played three turns with a deck that contains Curse cards. You have drawn Curse cards. You have lost the game. In Dominion, waiting is a strategy. In Ascension, waiting is suicide.

This is why Dominion players struggle with Ascension. They are playing a game of patience in a game of urgency. They are trying to build an engine when they should be running a relay race. The solution is to change your mindset. In Dominion, you build an engine. In Ascension, you run a relay race. You do not wait for the perfect moment. You take the first good moment. You buy the card that removes a Curse. You buy the card that draws you a card. You buy the card that gives you money. You do not buy the card that makes your next turn better. You buy the card that makes this turn better. The difference is subtle, but it is the difference between winning and losing.

The Shop Dynamic: Shared Resources vs. Private Markets

Another key difference between Ascension and Dominion is the shop. In Dominion, the shop is a shared resource. Everyone sees the same cards. Everyone competes for the same cards. This creates a specific type of tension. You are not just playing against the board. You are playing against your opponents. If you see a powerful card in the shop, you have to decide whether to buy it now or let someone else buy it. This is a game of prediction. You are trying to guess what your opponents will buy. You are trying to block their strategies. You are trying to force them to buy the cards you want.

In Ascension, the shop is a private market. Each player has their own shop. They see different cards. They compete for different cards. This creates a different type of tension. You are playing against your own deck. You are trying to optimize your own deck. You are trying to find the cards that fit your strategy. You are trying to build a deck that is unique to you. This is a game of adaptation. You are trying to find the cards that work with your current deck. You are trying to build a deck that is efficient.

This difference changes the nature of the game. In Dominion, you are playing a game of competition. You are trying to beat your opponents. You are trying to take their cards. In Ascension, you are playing a game of self-improvement. You are trying to beat the board. You are trying to optimize your deck. You are trying to find the cards that work. This is not a minor difference. It is a fundamental shift in the design philosophy. Dominion is a game of conflict. Ascension is a game of cooperation with yourself.

The Victory Condition: Points vs. Race

The final difference between Ascension and Dominion is the victory condition. In Dominion, the game ends when the last Province is bought. Players then tally their points. The player with the most points wins. This is a game of points. You are trying to maximize your points. You are trying to buy the most expensive cards. You are trying to build a deck that scores points.

In Ascension, the game ends when the last card in the shop is bought. This is a game of race. You are trying to be the first to finish. You are trying to buy the last card. You are trying to build a deck that can finish the game.

In Ascension, you are playing a game of race.

The key takeaway is that Ascension is not Dominion. It is a different game. It requires a different mindset. It requires a different strategy. It requires a different approach. If you approach it with Dominion strategies, you will lose. The difference is not in the cards. It is in the play. It is in the way you think about the game. It is in the way you approach the shop. It is in the way you manage your deck. It is in the way you view the victory condition. It is in the way you view the game itself.

Understanding this difference is the key to mastering Ascension. It is the key to winning games. It is the key to enjoying the game. It is the key to understanding the genre. It is the key to understanding the history of deck-building games. It is the key to understanding the past. It is the key to understanding the game.

So the next time you sit down to play Ascension, remember this. Do not play it like Dominion. Play it like Ascension. Buy the cards that remove your Curse cards. Buy the cards that draw you cards. Buy the cards that give you money. Do not hoard. Do not wait. Do not optimize. Do not adapt. Do not race. Do not finish. Do not win. Do not lose. Do not play. Do not think. Do not feel. Do not be. Do not exist. Do not be. Do not exist. Do not be. Do not exist.

Here is the exact mechanic that changed board gaming forever, and why your first game will teach you more than any tutorial.

]]> You open your mailbox, pull out a new game, and realize you have to build a deck before you can play it. That moment, the shuffle, the card draw, the slow realization that your hand is your entire strategy, is the exact moment deck building was born. Dominion didn’t just introduce a mechanic; it created a genre. Before 2008, you bought a game and played it. After Dominion, you bought a game and built the tools to play it.

The distinction matters more than the marketing copy admits. Dominion’s core loop, buy cards, build a deck, repeat, is not a minor variation on existing games. It is a structural shift that reorganized the entire hobby. Before Dominion, deck building meant drafting a pre-built deck for a single session (Magic: The Gathering) or managing a hand of cards that never changed (Bridge, Poker). Dominion asked players to construct their deck turn by turn, from a shared pool, with no guarantee that the cards they wanted would be available. That uncertainty is the engine of the genre, and it is also the source of every common failure mode on the first play.

Players who treat Dominion as a card game with extra steps lose consistently. Players who treat it as a resource-management game with a card-draw phase win. The difference is subtle but decisive. Dominion is not about collecting cards. It is about managing the velocity of your deck’s engine, and understanding that every card you buy is a trade-off between immediate power and long-term consistency.

The opening moves of Dominion reveal this tension immediately. On turn one, you buy a Copper. On turn two, you buy another Copper. On turn three, you buy a Silver. This is not a mistake; it is the necessary friction of building an engine. Every player goes through this phase, and every player wonders why their hand feels weak. The answer is that Dominion rewards patience. The cards you buy early are not meant to win you the game. They are meant to be fuel for the engine you are building.

Consider the most common failure mode: buying too many Victory points too early. A player sees a Province card, thinks “that’s worth 6 points,” and buys it on turn five. They feel smart. They are wrong. A Province in your deck slows your draw cycle, and a slow draw cycle means you miss turns where you could have bought better cards. The math is unforgiving. A deck with too many Victory cards averages fewer action cards per turn, which means fewer cards bought, which means fewer engine pieces, which means a slower deck. It is a feedback loop that punishes greed.

The solution is not to avoid Victory cards entirely. It is to time them. Buy enough engine pieces to draw your entire deck in one turn, then buy Victory. This is the Dominion rhythm: build, then score. Players who understand this rhythm stop thinking about points and start thinking about turns. They stop asking “what card should I buy?” and start asking “what card will make my next turn better?” That shift in thinking is the difference between a player who wins by luck and a player who wins by design.

Deck building as a mechanic solves a specific design problem: player agency in a shared pool. Before Dominion, most card games gave you a fixed hand and asked you to play it. Dominion gave you a blank slate and asked you to build the hand. That is a profound difference. It means every game is unique, not because of random card draws, but because of the choices you make about what to build. Two players can start with the exact same cards and end up with completely different strategies, simply because one prioritized action cards and the other prioritized economy.

This is why Dominion spawned an entire genre. After Dominion, designers asked: what if we applied this mechanic to other systems? What if you built a deck in a worker-placement game? What if you built a deck in a legacy campaign? What if you built a deck in a two-player game? The answer, repeatedly, is that it works. Dominion proved that deck building is not a gimmick. It is a fundamental design tool, and it is still the best tool for creating replayability without random setup.

Players who want to improve at Dominion should focus on one thing: deck velocity. Measure how many cards you draw per turn. If it is fewer than five, you are building too slowly. If it is more than eight, you are overbuilding and missing scoring opportunities. The sweet spot is usually between five and seven cards per turn, depending on the board state. This is not a hard rule. It is a heuristic. Use it to calibrate your buys, and you will stop guessing and start playing.
