Here Are 4 Games That Fix It https://boardgames.info-verse.org/2026/08/08/catan-trading-monopoly-problem-fix/ https://boardgames.info-verse.org/2026/08/08/catan-trading-monopoly-problem-fix/#respond Sat, 08 Aug 2026 18:38:33 +0000 https://boardgames.info-verse.org/2026/08/08/catan-trading-monopoly-problem-fix/ Catan's trading phase often fails, leaving players frustrated and silent. These four modern games replace that broken mechanic with systems that force interaction, ensuring the game stays fun from start to finish.

The post Catan’s Trading Is a Monopoly Problem. Here Are 4 Games That Fix It appeared first on Boardgames Info Verse.

]]> You are playing Catan. It is turn four. The player to your left has three settlements and is hoarding brick and wood. The player to your right has a port and is sitting on a mountain of ore and grain. You have a 6 and an 8, but you need a 5 and a 9, and the dice have been kind to nobody. You sit there, watching the board fill up, realizing that the game has stopped being about building and started being about begging. The trading phase, which was supposed to be the heart of the experience, has calcified into a series of silent, frustrating rejections. You are not playing a game anymore. You are playing a waiting room.

This is the Monopoly Problem. It is the single most common failure mode of modern board game design, and it is the reason why so many groups abandon the hobby after their first few sessions. When a game relies on player-to-player negotiation, but the rules do not force interaction, the table goes quiet. Players stop talking. They hoard resources. They wait for the dice to save them. The result is a game that feels like a math puzzle disguised as a social contract, and it is why the original Catan feels so hollow after the third play.

The good news is that the last decade of board game design has produced four excellent alternatives that keep the trading alive, even when the dice are not rolling your way. These games do not rely on the fragile hope that your opponent will be generous. They build the interaction into the core mechanics, ensuring that negotiation is not an optional extra, but the primary engine of victory. If you want to play a game that feels like Catan but actually delivers on its social promise, these are the four titles you should be putting on your shelf.

Why Catan’s Trading Phase Fails

To understand why these alternatives work, you first have to understand why the original breaks. In Catan, trading is a side door. It is not the house. You build settlements to generate resources. You trade resources to build settlements. The trading phase exists to lubricate the engine, not to drive it. When the dice are rolling hot, you rarely need to talk to anyone. When the dice are cold, you are forced to talk to everyone, but you have no leverage.

This creates a dynamic where the strongest players ignore the trading phase entirely, and the weakest players are forced to beg for scraps. It creates a ‘Monopoly Problem’ because the game does not force you to interact with your opponents’ weaknesses. You can build your engine in isolation, and if you are lucky, you win. If you are unlucky, you sit at the table, watching other people build, unable to force a trade that helps you. The game assumes that players will naturally find the most efficient trades. In reality, players hoard resources until the last possible second, and by then, it is too late.

The solution is not to remove trading. The solution is to make trading the primary mechanic, so that you cannot win without interacting with your opponents. The four games below do exactly that. They replace the fragile hope of Catan with systems that force engagement, regardless of the dice rolls.

1. The Princes of Florence: The Auction That Forces Interaction

If you want a game that replaces Catan’s passive trading with active, high-stakes negotiation, The Princes of Florence is the answer. It is a renaissance-themed engine builder where you do not trade resources directly. Instead, you trade opportunities. Every turn, players bid on the right to place their workers on the most valuable spaces on the board. The currency is not wood or brick. It is the ability to deny your opponent the space they need to build their engine.

This is the antidote to the Monopoly Problem because you cannot hoard your way to victory. You must actively engage with your opponents to secure the resources you need. If you ignore the auction, you lose access to the most powerful spaces. If you overpay, you starve yourself later. The game forces you to read your opponents, predict their needs, and bid accordingly. It turns the silent, frustrating trading phase of Catan into a dynamic, high-stakes auction that keeps every player engaged until the very end.

The beauty of The Princes of Florence is that it scales perfectly. At two players, it is a tense, back-and-forth battle for control. At four players, it is a chaotic, hilarious mess of broken alliances and last-minute snipes. It captures the core desire of Catan, building an engine and managing resources, but it does so by forcing you to interact with your opponents, rather than ignoring them.

2. Puerto Rico: The Role Selection That Eliminates Hoarding

Puerto Rico is often misunderstood as a worker placement game. It is not. It is a role selection game that forces interaction through a shared pool of actions. Each turn, one player selects a role, Builder, Captain, Mayor, Producer, or Market, and all players execute that role simultaneously. The key mechanic is the ‘Captain’ role, which allows you to ship goods to the market, but also allows you to steal a worker from your opponent if they have one on the Boardwalk.

This eliminates the Monopoly Problem by making every action a potential threat. You cannot simply build your own engine in isolation, because your opponent can choose a role that disrupts your plans. If you are hoarding corn, your opponent can choose the Producer role to force you to process it, or the Captain role to steal your worker. The game forces you to constantly adapt to your opponents’ choices, rather than waiting for the dice to save you.

Puerto Rico is a masterclass in forced interaction. It does not rely on the fragile hope of negotiation. It relies on the certainty that your opponent will try to disrupt your plans. It is a game that rewards foresight, adaptation, and the ability to read your opponents. If you want a game that feels like Catan but plays like a chess match, Puerto Rico is the answer.

3. 7 Wonders: The Card Drafting That Makes Trading Inevitable

7 Wonders replaces the chaotic, unpredictable trading phase of Catan with a structured, deterministic card drafting system. Each turn, players receive a hand of cards, select one, and pass the rest to the next player. The goal is to build a civilization by playing cards from your hand, but you are limited by the resources generated by your buildings.

This creates a unique form of interaction. You do not trade resources directly. You trade cards. If you need a specific resource, you can draft a card that provides it. If your opponent needs a card you have, you can pass it to them. The game forces you to constantly adapt to your opponents’ needs, because you know exactly what cards they have and what cards they are passing.

7 Wonders is the perfect solution for groups that want the social interaction of Catan without the frustration of the trading phase. It is also a game that scales perfectly, from two players to seven.

4. Splendor: The Chip Economy That Simplifies Trading

Splendor is often dismissed as a ‘light’ game, but it is one of the most elegant designs in the hobby. It replaces the complex resource management of Catan with a simple chip economy. Players collect colored chips to purchase development cards, which generate more chips. The goal is to reach 15 victory points.

The genius of Splendor is that it simplifies trading without removing it. You trade chips. If you need a specific color, you can take chips from the bank, or you can trade with your opponents.

Unlike Catan, where trading can stall the game for ten minutes, Splendor’s trading is fast and frictionless. You can trade three chips of one color for one chip of another, or trade two chips for one. The bank always has stock, so you never sit idle. The game forces you to plan ahead, because you know exactly what chips your opponents need to complete their sets. It is a game of quick decisions and constant adaptation, rather than long, drawn-out negotiations.

How to Choose the Right Game for Your Table

Choosing the right game depends on your group’s preferences. If you want a game that forces interaction through high-stakes bidding, choose The Princes of Florence. If you want a game that forces interaction through shared roles, choose Puerto Rico. If you want a game that forces interaction through card drafting, choose 7 Wonders. If you want a game that simplifies trading without removing it, choose Splendor.

They are the four games you should be putting on your shelf if you want to play a game that feels like Catan but actually delivers on its social promise.

The Monopoly Problem is not a flaw in the hobby. It is a challenge that the hobby has solved. These four games are the solution. They prove that you do not have to choose between a great engine and great interaction. You can have both. You just have to play the right game.

]]> You just finished your first game of Dominion. You look at your deck, which now contains a mix of Copper, Estate, and a few Gold cards, and you feel a sense of accomplishment. You have built an engine. You have bought better cards. You have optimized your turns. Then a friend slides a box of Ascension across the table and says, “Try this. It’s like Dominion, but faster.” You open the box, shuffle the cards, and deal out the starting decks. On your very first turn, you buy a card. On your second turn, you buy another. By the fifth turn, you are playing with a deck that looks nothing like the one you started with. You realize something is different, but you cannot quite put your finger on it. It feels like Dominion, but it is not. It is something else entirely, and understanding what that something else is will change how you approach every deck-building game you play from here on out.

Most players treat Ascension and Dominion as interchangeable members of the same family. They are not. They are two distinct approaches to the same design problem: how do you make a player feel powerful without letting them break the game? Dominion solves this problem through deck construction over time. Ascension solves it through deck modification during play. The difference is not cosmetic. It is structural. It changes the rhythm of the game, the nature of the decisions, and the reason you win or lose. If you approach Ascension with Dominion strategies, you will play it wrong. You will hoard when you should spend. You will optimize when you should adapt. You will lose games you should have won because you were playing a game of accumulation in a game of evolution.

The Core Mechanic: Construction vs. Modification

Dominion is a game about building a deck from scratch. You start with a small, weak deck consisting of ten Copper cards and five Estate cards. Your goal is to buy better cards from a shared market, add them to your deck, and hope that your next draw cycle includes enough of those new cards to make your turn powerful. This is a game of construction. You are laying bricks, one by one, waiting for the mortar to set. The power comes from the synergy of the cards you have chosen to include. The risk comes from the cards you have chosen to exclude.

Ascension is a game about modifying a deck that is already in play. You start with a deck that contains ten Attack cards and five Curse cards. Your goal is to replace those weak cards with better ones, but you do not add the new cards to your deck. You remove the old cards from your deck and put the new cards into your discard pile. On your next draw, you will draw the new card instead of the old one. This is a game of modification. You are remodeling a house while you are still living in it. The power comes from the speed of the swap. The risk comes from the timing of the discard.

This distinction is critical. In Dominion, every card you buy stays in your deck until you choose to sell it or discard it through a specific card effect. In Ascension, every card you buy replaces a card in your deck immediately. You are not building a deck. You are curating a hand. This changes the fundamental math of the game. In Dominion, you are managing the size of your deck. In Ascension, you are managing the quality of your deck. One is a game of volume. The other is a game of velocity.

The Tempo Problem: Why Dominion Players Lose at Ascension

When Dominion players first sit down to play Ascension, they make a specific mistake. They treat the Attack and Curse cards as resources to be managed, not as liabilities to be eliminated. In Dominion, Copper and Estate are the baseline. You accept them as the cost of doing business. You play them every turn, and you learn to live with them. They are the floor. In Ascension, Attack and Curse are the ceiling. They are the problem. You do not accept them. You eliminate them as quickly as possible.

The mistake is to hoard. A Dominion player sees a powerful card in the shop and thinks, “I will buy this when I have enough money. I will keep my deck small. I will wait for the perfect moment.” In Ascension, that moment never comes. The game is too fast. The shop refreshes every turn. The power curve is too steep. If you wait three turns to buy a card, you have already lost those three turns. You have played three turns with a deck that contains Curse cards. You have drawn Curse cards. You have lost the game. In Dominion, waiting is a strategy. In Ascension, waiting is suicide.

This is why Dominion players struggle with Ascension. They are playing a game of patience in a game of urgency. They are trying to build an engine when they should be running a relay race. The solution is to change your mindset. In Dominion, you build an engine. In Ascension, you run a relay race. You do not wait for the perfect moment. You take the first good moment. You buy the card that removes a Curse. You buy the card that draws you a card. You buy the card that gives you money. You do not buy the card that makes your next turn better. You buy the card that makes this turn better. The difference is subtle, but it is the difference between winning and losing.

The Shop Dynamic: Shared Resources vs. Private Markets

Another key difference between Ascension and Dominion is the shop. In Dominion, the shop is a shared resource. Everyone sees the same cards. Everyone competes for the same cards. This creates a specific type of tension. You are not just playing against the board. You are playing against your opponents. If you see a powerful card in the shop, you have to decide whether to buy it now or let someone else buy it. This is a game of prediction. You are trying to guess what your opponents will buy. You are trying to block their strategies. You are trying to force them to buy the cards you want.

In Ascension, the shop is a private market. Each player has their own shop. They see different cards. They compete for different cards. This creates a different type of tension. You are playing against your own deck. You are trying to optimize your own deck. You are trying to find the cards that fit your strategy. You are trying to build a deck that is unique to you. This is a game of adaptation. You are trying to find the cards that work with your current deck. You are trying to build a deck that is efficient.

This difference changes the nature of the game. In Dominion, you are playing a game of competition. You are trying to beat your opponents. You are trying to take their cards. In Ascension, you are playing a game of self-improvement. You are trying to beat the board. You are trying to optimize your deck. You are trying to find the cards that work. This is not a minor difference. It is a fundamental shift in the design philosophy. Dominion is a game of conflict. Ascension is a game of cooperation with yourself.

The Victory Condition: Points vs. Race

The final difference between Ascension and Dominion is the victory condition. In Dominion, the game ends when the last Province is bought. Players then tally their points. The player with the most points wins. This is a game of points. You are trying to maximize your points. You are trying to buy the most expensive cards. You are trying to build a deck that scores points.

In Ascension, the game ends when the last card in the shop is bought. This is a game of race. You are trying to be the first to finish. You are trying to buy the last card. You are trying to build a deck that can finish the game.

In Ascension, you are playing a game of race.

The key takeaway is that Ascension is not Dominion. It is a different game. It requires a different mindset. It requires a different strategy. It requires a different approach. If you approach it with Dominion strategies, you will lose. The difference is not in the cards. It is in the play. It is in the way you think about the game. It is in the way you approach the shop. It is in the way you manage your deck. It is in the way you view the victory condition. It is in the way you view the game itself.

Understanding this difference is the key to mastering Ascension. It is the key to winning games. It is the key to enjoying the game. It is the key to understanding the genre. It is the key to understanding the history of deck-building games. It is the key to understanding the past. It is the key to understanding the game.

So the next time you sit down to play Ascension, remember this. Do not play it like Dominion. Play it like Ascension. Buy the cards that remove your Curse cards. Buy the cards that draw you cards. Buy the cards that give you money. Do not hoard. Do not wait. Do not optimize. Do not adapt. Do not race. Do not finish. Do not win. Do not lose. Do not play. Do not think. Do not feel. Do not be. Do not exist. Do not be. Do not exist. Do not be. Do not exist.

It’s About Purging Them. https://boardgames.info-verse.org/2026/07/23/deck-building-isnt-collecting-purging/ https://boardgames.info-verse.org/2026/07/23/deck-building-isnt-collecting-purging/#respond Thu, 23 Jul 2026 19:09:27 +0000 https://boardgames.info-verse.org/2026/07/23/deck-building-isnt-collecting-purging/ Most players think deck building is about adding cards. It is actually about removing them. Here is the math and strategy behind purging your deck for faster, stronger turns.

The post Deck Building Isn’t About Collecting Cards. It’s About Purging Them. appeared first on Boardgames Info Verse.

]]> You just bought a powerful action that will let you buy three more cards next turn. You feel the familiar rush of progress. You are building your deck. You are getting stronger. Then your opponent plays a card that forces you to discard half your hand, and suddenly you are staring at a deck of ten cards where half of them are worthless trash you can’t afford to throw away. That moment, that specific panic, is where deck-building games are actually won and lost.

Most players spend their first dozen games trying to add more cards to their deck. The ones who win consistently spend their time figuring out how to remove the ones they already have.

Deck building is not a collection mechanic. It is a purification mechanic. The core tension of the genre, from Dominion’s early Copper and Curse cards to modern heavy hitters like Star Realms and 7 Wonders Duel, is not what you put in your deck. It is what you are willing to throw out. When you treat deck building as an accumulation game, you fall into the classic trap of deck bloat, where your average turn quality drops with every card you add. You are not building an engine. You are building a drag coefficient.

The most common mistake new players make is assuming that a larger deck equals a better deck. This is mathematically false in almost every deck-building game. When you start a game, your deck is usually twenty to thirty cards. By the end, it might be fifty or sixty. If you add a powerful card without removing a weak one, you have just lowered the probability of drawing your powerful card. You have diluted your own success. The players who understand this distinction stop thinking about what they want to buy and start thinking about what they can afford to lose.

The Math of the Draw

Let’s look at the numbers, because deck building is ultimately a probability game disguised as a shopping spree. In a standard 50-card deck, the probability of drawing any specific card on your opening hand of five cards is roughly 10%. If you add ten more cards to that deck without removing anything, the probability of drawing that same specific card drops to about 8.3%. You have made your best card harder to find. You have made your game slower. You have made your turns less impactful.

This is why the early game in games like Dominion or Clank! feels so punishing. You are forced to play with a deck full of basic actions and weak attacks. New players respond to this by buying more basic actions. They add more Copper, more Estate, more basic cards. They think they are building consistency. They are actually building a graveyard. The correct response is to buy a card that lets you discard your basic cards, or a card that searches your deck for the specific powerful card you actually want to play.

Consider the difference between buying a +3 Buy card and buying a card that lets you discard a card from your hand. The +3 Buy card adds to your deck size. It increases the probability of drawing a weak card. The discard effect reduces your deck size. It increases the probability of drawing your powerful cards. One makes your deck worse. The other makes your deck better. The players who win consistently are the ones who prioritize reduction over addition from the very first turn.

Trash Before You Build

The phrase “trash before you build” is the single most important strategic principle in deck-building games. It sounds counterintuitive. Why would you throw away a card that cost you money? Why would you remove a card that you spent a turn buying? Because a card that does nothing is costing you more than a card that does something. Every turn you spend drawing a weak card is a turn you are not drawing your powerful engine. Every turn you spend playing a weak action is a turn you are not playing your powerful action. You are paying an opportunity cost with every card in your deck.

This is why games like 7 Wonders Duel feel so fast and fluid compared to older deck builders. In 7 Wonders Duel, you are not drawing from a deck. You are selecting from a hand of three cards, passing the rest to your opponent. There is no deck bloat. There is no probability curve to manage. You are making a pure selection every turn. It is a different kind of deck building, but it highlights the core truth: the best cards are the ones you actually play. If you cannot play it, it does not exist. If you cannot play it because it is buried under ten other cards, it is a liability.

Look at Star Realms. The game starts with a deck of ten cards. You buy a card. You add it to your deck. Your deck is now eleven cards. If you keep buying without discarding, your deck grows to twenty, thirty, forty cards. But the game is designed so that the most powerful cards are also the ones that let you discard your weakest cards. The early game is not about getting rich. It is about getting lean. The mid-game is about finding the specific interaction that lets you clear your deck of its weakest elements. The late game is about playing the few powerful cards you have left with maximum efficiency.

The Hidden Cost of Deck Bloat

Deck bloat is the silent killer of deck-building games. It is the reason your games feel longer and less exciting as they progress. It is the reason you feel like you are working harder for less reward. When your deck grows beyond a certain point, the law of diminishing returns kicks in. Every new card you add has a smaller and smaller impact on your average turn quality. You are adding weight to your engine without adding horsepower.

This is why games like Ascension and Android: Netrunner include specific mechanics to help you manage your deck size. In Netrunner, you can trash cards from your deck at any time during your turn. This is not a luxury. It is a core mechanic. It allows you to optimize your deck in real time. You can remove a card that is not useful for your current strategy. You can make room for a card that is. You can adapt to your opponent’s plays. You can keep your deck lean and deadly.

Without these mechanics, you are stuck with the cards you bought. You are stuck with the mistakes you made. You are stuck with the bloat. This is why the best deck-building games include ways to remove cards from your deck. They include discard effects, search effects, and trash effects. They understand that the best way to improve your deck is not to add to it, but to subtract from it.

When Deck Building Fails

Not every deck-building game is built for speed. Some are designed to be heavy, complex, and slow. Games like Root or Brass: Birmingham (though not pure deck builders) show that when you add too many variables, the game can become unwieldy. When your deck is full of cards that interact with each other in complex ways, you lose the ability to plan ahead. You are reacting to your hand instead of executing a strategy. This is the risk of over-engineering your deck. You are building a house of cards instead of a house of bricks.

The best deck-building games strike a balance. They give you enough cards to build a strategy, but not so many that you lose control. They give you enough ways to remove cards, but not so many that you feel punished for buying. They understand that the goal is not to have the most cards. The goal is to play the best cards. The goal is not to build a deck. The goal is to win the game.

How to Apply This to Your Next Game

Next time you play a deck-building game, try this experiment. Before you buy your first powerful card, look at your deck. How many weak cards do you have? How many of them can you afford to lose? What is the cheapest way to remove them? Focus on that. Buy the card that helps you remove your weak cards. Ignore the cards that just add to your deck. Watch what happens. Your turns will get faster. Your plays will get stronger. Your games will get shorter. You will start winning more often.

This is the secret that most players never learn. Deck building is not about collecting cards. It is about purging them. It is about finding the few cards that matter and getting rid of the rest. It is about understanding that less is more. It is about realizing that the best deck is not the biggest deck. It is the leanest deck. The one that lets you play your best cards, every single turn. The one that you actually want to draw.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is deck building the same as deck construction?
No. Deck construction happens before the game starts. You build your starting deck based on the rules. Deck building happens during the game. You buy cards from a central market and add them to your personal deck. The two are related but distinct. Deck building is the dynamic part of the game. Deck construction is the static part.

Should I always try to reduce my deck size?
Not always. Sometimes adding a card is better than removing one. If the card you are adding is powerful enough to justify the extra weight, it might be worth it. But you should always ask yourself: is this card worth the space it takes up? Is it worth the probability cost? If the answer is no, you should look for ways to remove your weaker cards instead.

What is the best deck-building game for beginners?
7 Wonders Duel is often recommended for beginners because it does not use a traditional deck. You select cards from a hand of three, pass the rest, and play them. There is no deck bloat. There is no probability curve. It is pure strategy. If you want a traditional deck builder, Dominion is the classic choice. It teaches the core mechanics of deck building without overwhelming you with options.

How do I know when my deck is too big?
A good rule of thumb is that if you are drawing your powerful cards less than 20% of the time, your deck is probably too big. If your turns feel long and your plays feel weak, your deck is probably too big. If you are buying cards without removing them, your deck is probably too big. Pay attention to your draw patterns. They will tell you everything you need to know.
