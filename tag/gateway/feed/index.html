]]> Map size is a lie. It is the single most effective marketing trick in modern board gaming, designed to sell complexity to parents who just want a quiet hour. A smaller box does not mean a simpler game. The real differentiator is the era of design, not the physical size of the components. A 1970s-era game like the original Ticket to Ride Europe shrinks the board but keeps the 1970s cognitive load: hidden information, complex scoring, and multi-layered decision trees. A true modern gateway game for kids, like Ticket to Ride: First Journey or Dinosaur Island, strips that load away entirely. If you want a game that keeps children engaged without boring the adults, you have to stop looking at the box size and start looking at the design decade.

When you sit down with a six-year-old and an eight-year-old, you are not looking for a strategy game. You are looking for a shared experience that does not require a rulebook to function. The mistake most parents make is buying a game that looks like a gateway game but plays like one. They buy a game with a small map, assuming the map size dictates the complexity. It does not. The map size dictates the play time. The design philosophy dictates the cognitive load.

The 1970s Design Trap: Why Small Maps Still Feel Heavy

Let us look at the original Ticket to Ride, released in 2004, and its European expansion, released in 2005. These games are the gold standard for gateway board games. They are widely recommended for families. They are also often too complex for young children, regardless of how small the map becomes. The European map is smaller, yes, but it introduces a new mechanic: tunnels and ferries. These are not just aesthetic changes. They are cognitive load multipliers. A tunnel requires you to hold a specific color in reserve, even if you do not need it for your current route. A ferry requires you to hold a specific color for a specific number of turns. For a child under ten, this is not a strategy. It is a memory test disguised as a game.

The 1970s design philosophy, inherited by these 2000s games, prioritizes player interaction and hidden information. In Ticket to Ride, you are actively blocking your opponent. You are looking at their tickets and trying to guess their next move. This is excellent for adults. It is exhausting for children. Children do not want to block. They want to build. They want to see their progress. They want to feel like they are winning, even if they are not. When you force a child to play a 1970s-style game, you are asking them to engage in a social contract they do not yet understand. They will get frustrated when their route is blocked. They will get confused when they have to hold a card they do not need. They will eventually stop playing, and you will be left with a half-finished game and a disappointed child.

This is not a failure of the child. It is a failure of the design. The game was not built for them. It was built for adults who enjoy the friction of interaction. If you want a game that is easy to learn and play with children, you must look for games that were designed with a different philosophy: the modern gateway game. These games prioritize clear goals, visible progress, and minimal hidden information. They are not about blocking. They are about building. They are not about guessing. They are about executing.

The Modern Gateway: What Actually Works for Kids

So, what does a modern gateway game for kids actually look like? It looks like a game where the rules are simple, the components are large, and the goals are clear. It looks like a game where the child can look at the board and understand exactly what they need to do. It looks like a game where the adult can sit back and enjoy the game, rather than spending the entire session explaining the rules.

Consider Dinosaur Island. This game is a modern gateway game for kids. It is simple, it is fun, and it is engaging for adults. The goal is simple: collect dinosaur cards and build your island. The components are large and colorful. The rules are easy to learn. The game is fast. It is also deeply engaging for adults, who enjoy the strategic depth of card management and resource allocation. It is a true gateway game, because it bridges the gap between simple children’s games and complex adult games. It is about building. It is about executing. It is the perfect game for a family with young children.

Another excellent example is Ticket to Ride: First Journey. This game is a simplified version of the original Ticket to Ride. It uses a smaller map, yes, but more importantly, it simplifies the mechanics. There are no tunnels. There are no ferries. There are no hidden tickets. The goal is simple: connect cities. It is also engaging for adults, who enjoy the strategic depth of route planning and resource management.

These games share a common design philosophy: they prioritize the child’s experience. They do not try to trick the child. They do not try to confuse the child. They do not try to frustrate the child. They try to engage the child. They try to make the child feel like they are winning. They try to make the child feel like they are part of the game. This is the hallmark of a true gateway game for kids. It is about the design philosophy.

How to Choose the Right Game for Your Family

When you are shopping for a new board game for your family, do not look at the map size. Look at the design philosophy. Look for games that are simple, engaging, and fun. Look for games that prioritize the child’s experience. Look for games that are easy to learn and play. Look for games that are engaging for adults. Look for games that are true gateway games.

Here is a quick checklist to help you choose the right game for your family:

  • Simple Rules: The game should be easy to learn in under 15 minutes. If it takes longer, it is not a gateway game.
  • Clear Goals: The goal should be clear and visible. The child should be able to look at the board and understand exactly what they need to do.
  • Large Components: The components should be large and colorful. Small pieces are frustrating for young children.
  • Minimal Hidden Information: The game should not rely on hidden information or bluffing. Children do not understand these mechanics.
  • Visible Progress: The child should be able to see their progress.
  • Engaging for Adults: The game should be engaging for adults. If the adult is bored, the child will be bored.

If a game meets all of these criteria, it is likely a true gateway game for kids. It will be easy to learn and play with children. It will keep children engaged without boring the adults. It will be a success.

When a Gateway Game Fails

Even the best gateway games can fail if you do not manage expectations. A child who is used to video games may find a board game slow. A child who is used to simple children’s games may find a gateway game complex. A child who is used to competitive games may find a cooperative game frustrating. It is important to manage these expectations and to be patient. It is also important to be willing to stop playing if the game is not working. There is no shame in putting a game away and trying something else. The goal is to have fun, not to win.

Another common failure mode is playing too many games in one session. A child’s attention span is limited. If you play three games in one session, the child will be exhausted by the third game. It is better to play one game well than three games poorly. It is also better to play shorter games. A 30-minute game is better than a 90-minute game. A 30-minute game is easier to learn, easier to play, and easier to stop. It is also more likely to be fun.

Finally, do not be afraid to simplify a game. If a game is too complex for your child, simplify it. Remove some of the rules. Remove some of the components. Remove some of the options. The goal is to have fun, not to follow the rules exactly. If you can make a game simpler and still have fun, you have succeeded. If you cannot, you have chosen the wrong game.

FAQ

What is the best board game for kids under 6?

For children under six, the best board games are those with minimal rules and large components. Games like Outfoxed!, Orchard Toys’ Fox in the Forest, and Candy Land are excellent choices. They are easy to learn, easy to play, and engaging for young children. They do not require reading or complex strategy. They are purely about fun and interaction.

Is Ticket to Ride too hard for kids?

The original Ticket to Ride can be too hard for children under ten. The tunnels and ferries add a layer of complexity that young children struggle with. However, Ticket to Ride: First Journey is specifically designed for younger players. It removes the tunnels and ferries, simplifies the scoring, and uses larger components. It is an excellent gateway game for children aged six and up.

How do I know if a game is a true gateway game?

A true gateway game is one that is easy to learn, easy to play, and engaging for both children and adults. It has simple rules, clear goals, and visible progress. It does not rely on hidden information or complex strategy. It is designed to bridge the gap between simple children’s games and complex adult games.

What are some other good gateway games for families?

Other excellent gateway games for families include Dinosaur Island, King of Tokyo, Carcassonne, and Azul. These games are simple, engaging, and fun for both children and adults.

Here Are 4 Games That Fix It https://boardgames.info-verse.org/2026/08/08/catan-trading-monopoly-problem-fix/ https://boardgames.info-verse.org/2026/08/08/catan-trading-monopoly-problem-fix/#respond Sat, 08 Aug 2026 18:38:33 +0000 https://boardgames.info-verse.org/2026/08/08/catan-trading-monopoly-problem-fix/ Catan's trading phase often fails, leaving players frustrated and silent. These four modern games replace that broken mechanic with systems that force interaction, ensuring the game stays fun from start to finish.

]]> You are playing Catan. It is turn four. The player to your left has three settlements and is hoarding brick and wood. The player to your right has a port and is sitting on a mountain of ore and grain. You have a 6 and an 8, but you need a 5 and a 9, and the dice have been kind to nobody. You sit there, watching the board fill up, realizing that the game has stopped being about building and started being about begging. The trading phase, which was supposed to be the heart of the experience, has calcified into a series of silent, frustrating rejections. You are not playing a game anymore. You are playing a waiting room.

This is the Monopoly Problem. It is the single most common failure mode of modern board game design, and it is the reason why so many groups abandon the hobby after their first few sessions. When a game relies on player-to-player negotiation, but the rules do not force interaction, the table goes quiet. Players stop talking. They hoard resources. They wait for the dice to save them. The result is a game that feels like a math puzzle disguised as a social contract, and it is why the original Catan feels so hollow after the third play.

The good news is that the last decade of board game design has produced four excellent alternatives that keep the trading alive, even when the dice are not rolling your way. These games do not rely on the fragile hope that your opponent will be generous. They build the interaction into the core mechanics, ensuring that negotiation is not an optional extra, but the primary engine of victory. If you want to play a game that feels like Catan but actually delivers on its social promise, these are the four titles you should be putting on your shelf.

Why Catan’s Trading Phase Fails

To understand why these alternatives work, you first have to understand why the original breaks. In Catan, trading is a side door. It is not the house. You build settlements to generate resources. You trade resources to build settlements. The trading phase exists to lubricate the engine, not to drive it. When the dice are rolling hot, you rarely need to talk to anyone. When the dice are cold, you are forced to talk to everyone, but you have no leverage.

This creates a dynamic where the strongest players ignore the trading phase entirely, and the weakest players are forced to beg for scraps. It creates a ‘Monopoly Problem’ because the game does not force you to interact with your opponents’ weaknesses. You can build your engine in isolation, and if you are lucky, you win. If you are unlucky, you sit at the table, watching other people build, unable to force a trade that helps you. The game assumes that players will naturally find the most efficient trades. In reality, players hoard resources until the last possible second, and by then, it is too late.

The solution is not to remove trading. The solution is to make trading the primary mechanic, so that you cannot win without interacting with your opponents. The four games below do exactly that. They replace the fragile hope of Catan with systems that force engagement, regardless of the dice rolls.

1. The Princes of Florence: The Auction That Forces Interaction

If you want a game that replaces Catan’s passive trading with active, high-stakes negotiation, The Princes of Florence is the answer. It is a renaissance-themed engine builder where you do not trade resources directly. Instead, you trade opportunities. Every turn, players bid on the right to place their workers on the most valuable spaces on the board. The currency is not wood or brick. It is the ability to deny your opponent the space they need to build their engine.

This is the antidote to the Monopoly Problem because you cannot hoard your way to victory. You must actively engage with your opponents to secure the resources you need. If you ignore the auction, you lose access to the most powerful spaces. If you overpay, you starve yourself later. The game forces you to read your opponents, predict their needs, and bid accordingly. It turns the silent, frustrating trading phase of Catan into a dynamic, high-stakes auction that keeps every player engaged until the very end.

The beauty of The Princes of Florence is that it scales perfectly. At two players, it is a tense, back-and-forth battle for control. At four players, it is a chaotic, hilarious mess of broken alliances and last-minute snipes. It captures the core desire of Catan, building an engine and managing resources, but it does so by forcing you to interact with your opponents, rather than ignoring them.

2. Puerto Rico: The Role Selection That Eliminates Hoarding

Puerto Rico is often misunderstood as a worker placement game. It is not. It is a role selection game that forces interaction through a shared pool of actions. Each turn, one player selects a role, Builder, Captain, Mayor, Producer, or Market, and all players execute that role simultaneously. The key mechanic is the ‘Captain’ role, which allows you to ship goods to the market, but also allows you to steal a worker from your opponent if they have one on the Boardwalk.

This eliminates the Monopoly Problem by making every action a potential threat. You cannot simply build your own engine in isolation, because your opponent can choose a role that disrupts your plans. If you are hoarding corn, your opponent can choose the Producer role to force you to process it, or the Captain role to steal your worker. The game forces you to constantly adapt to your opponents’ choices, rather than waiting for the dice to save you.

Puerto Rico is a masterclass in forced interaction. It does not rely on the fragile hope of negotiation. It relies on the certainty that your opponent will try to disrupt your plans. It is a game that rewards foresight, adaptation, and the ability to read your opponents. If you want a game that feels like Catan but plays like a chess match, Puerto Rico is the answer.

3. 7 Wonders: The Card Drafting That Makes Trading Inevitable

7 Wonders replaces the chaotic, unpredictable trading phase of Catan with a structured, deterministic card drafting system. Each turn, players receive a hand of cards, select one, and pass the rest to the next player. The goal is to build a civilization by playing cards from your hand, but you are limited by the resources generated by your buildings.

This creates a unique form of interaction. You do not trade resources directly. You trade cards. If you need a specific resource, you can draft a card that provides it. If your opponent needs a card you have, you can pass it to them. The game forces you to constantly adapt to your opponents’ needs, because you know exactly what cards they have and what cards they are passing.

7 Wonders is the perfect solution for groups that want the social interaction of Catan without the frustration of the trading phase. It is also a game that scales perfectly, from two players to seven.

4. Splendor: The Chip Economy That Simplifies Trading

Splendor is often dismissed as a ‘light’ game, but it is one of the most elegant designs in the hobby. It replaces the complex resource management of Catan with a simple chip economy. Players collect colored chips to purchase development cards, which generate more chips. The goal is to reach 15 victory points.

The genius of Splendor is that it simplifies trading without removing it. You trade chips. If you need a specific color, you can take chips from the bank, or you can trade with your opponents.

Unlike Catan, where trading can stall the game for ten minutes, Splendor’s trading is fast and frictionless. You can trade three chips of one color for one chip of another, or trade two chips for one. The bank always has stock, so you never sit idle. The game forces you to plan ahead, because you know exactly what chips your opponents need to complete their sets. It is a game of quick decisions and constant adaptation, rather than long, drawn-out negotiations.

How to Choose the Right Game for Your Table

Choosing the right game depends on your group’s preferences. If you want a game that forces interaction through high-stakes bidding, choose The Princes of Florence. If you want a game that forces interaction through shared roles, choose Puerto Rico. If you want a game that forces interaction through card drafting, choose 7 Wonders. If you want a game that simplifies trading without removing it, choose Splendor.

They are the four games you should be putting on your shelf if you want to play a game that feels like Catan but actually delivers on its social promise.

The Monopoly Problem is not a flaw in the hobby. It is a challenge that the hobby has solved. These four games are the solution. They prove that you do not have to choose between a great engine and great interaction. You can have both. You just have to play the right game.

]]> Two people sitting across a board game table do not play a zero-sum game. They play a cooperative puzzle wearing a competitive mask. The moment one player tries to outmaneuver the other, the game breaks. The board state becomes impossible to resolve, the turns drag, and the fun evaporates. This is not a failure of the couple’s relationship. It is a failure of the game’s design to align with the player count.

When you look for the best 2 player board games for couples, you are not looking for a contest. You are looking for a shared experience where the board itself is the opponent. The best games for two people do not pit players against each other; they pit players against a system. They require communication, shared failure, and a willingness to sacrifice individual points for collective survival. If you treat a two-player game as a competition, you will lose. If you treat it as a shared puzzle, you will find the strategy that actually works.

The Two-Player Paradox: Why Competition Fails

Most board games are designed for three or more players. In a three-player game, Player A can block Player B while Player C focuses on scoring. The dynamic is fluid. In a two-player game, Player A’s move is Player B’s direct reality. There is no third party to absorb the impact. Every action is a direct counter-action. This creates a feedback loop of denial. If Player A takes a resource, Player B cannot take it. If Player A blocks a path, Player B cannot use it.

This is why standard competitive games fail at two players. The game becomes a game of “who can stop the other from winning” rather than “who can win.” The strategy shifts from optimization to obstruction. Players stop trying to build their own engine and start trying to break the other person’s. The result is a stagnant, negative-sum game where both players end up with fewer points than they could have had if they had simply worked together.

Shared failure is the antidote. When a game is designed for two players, the best ones build in mechanics that force cooperation. They create a shared goal that both players must achieve. If one player fails, both players fail. This aligns the incentives. It turns the game into a puzzle where the two players are the solution. The board is the obstacle. The dice are the randomizer. The players are the constant.

Cooperative Games That Feel Like Competition

Not all two-player games are cooperative. Some are purely competitive. Some are asymmetric. The best games for couples fall into a third category: games that feel competitive but are structurally cooperative. They allow players to pursue different goals, but those goals are constrained by a shared timer or a shared resource pool. If one player goes too far ahead, they break the game. If one player falls too far behind, they drag the other down.

Consider 7 Wonders Duel. It is a card drafting game. It is also a race. Each player builds a civilization. They draft cards to build wonders, develop science, and expand their city. The game ends when one player completes their third age. The player with the most points wins. On the surface, it is a competition. In practice, it is a race against the clock. If Player A moves too fast, Player B cannot catch up. If Player A moves too slow, Player B wins by default. The strategy is not to beat Player B. The strategy is to pace yourself so that Player B cannot catch you, but you do not leave them so far behind that they cannot catch up.

This is the core of the best 2 player board games for couples. It is a balancing act. It requires you to watch your opponent’s progress, not to stop them, but to calibrate your own. You are not trying to win. You are trying to stay in the game. The moment you stop trying to win, you start trying to stay in the game. The moment you start trying to stay in the game, you start playing the board, not the person.

Asymmetric Games: Playing Different Roles, Same Goal

Asymmetric games give each player a different set of tools, rules, or goals. In Root, the Marauders play a clock. They score points by controlling cities. The Birds play a war game. They score points by destroying the Marauders. The Vagabond plays a puzzle game. They score points by helping the underdog. The game is not about who has the best strategy. It is about how the strategies interact.

When played by two people, Root becomes a study in tension. The Marauder wants to expand. The Birds want to stop them. The Vagabond wants to survive. The game is not about winning. It is about creating a board state that is interesting. If one player wins too easily, the game is boring. If one player loses too easily, the game is frustrating. The best games for couples are games where the loss is shared.

This is why asymmetric games are so popular with couples. They allow each player to play their own game, while still being part of the same game. They create a dynamic where communication is essential. You cannot win alone. You cannot lose alone. You win together, or you lose together. The board is the common language. The dice are the common enemy. The players are the common solution.

Cooperative Games: The Board Is the Opponent

Cooperative games are the purest form of the two-player experience. There is no winner. There is no loser. There is only the board. If the players win, they win. If the players lose, they lose. The game is a puzzle. The players are the solution.

Consider Pandemic. It is a game about saving the world. Four players work together to cure four diseases. They move around the board, treating outbreaks, building research stations, and discovering cures. The game is not about who cures the most diseases. It is about curing all four diseases before the diseases cure themselves. The game is a race against time. The game is a race against randomness. The game is a race against each other.

When played by two people, Pandemic becomes a study in trust. You cannot save everyone. You cannot cure everything. You have to choose. You have to trust your partner to make the right choice. You have to trust your partner to make the choice you would not make. It is a test of trust. It is a test of communication. It is a test of shared failure.

How to Choose the Right Game for Your Table

Choosing the right game for your table is not about complexity. It is about compatibility. It is about finding a game that matches your communication style, your tolerance for randomness, and your desire for shared failure. If you like to argue, play a competitive game. If you like to collaborate, play a cooperative game. If you like to balance, play a two-player game.

The best games for couples are games that allow you to play your own game, while still being part of the same game. They are games that turn the board into a puzzle and the players into the solution. They are games that make you laugh, make you think, and make you feel like a team.

If you are looking for the best 2 player board games for couples, start with a game that has a shared goal. Start with a game that has a shared timer. Start with a game that has a shared resource pool. Start with a game that has a shared failure state. Start with a game that makes you feel like a team. That is the strategy. That is the secret. That is the point.

FAQ

What is the best 2 player board game for beginners?

7 Wonders Duel is the best entry point. It is a card drafting game that is easy to learn, hard to master, and perfectly balanced for two players. It teaches you how to pace yourself, how to read your opponent, and how to stay in the game.

Are cooperative games better for couples?

Cooperative games are not inherently better. They are different. They remove the competition and replace it with collaboration. They are ideal for couples who want to work together. They are less ideal for couples who want to compete. Choose based on your relationship dynamic.

How do I handle losing in a two-player game?

By treating the loss as shared. By recognizing that the board is the opponent. By understanding that the game is a puzzle, not a contest. By focusing on the next game, not the last one. By celebrating the shared failure, not the individual victory.

What is the most replayable 2 player game?

Root is the most replayable. The asymmetric factions create a different experience every time. The board state is never the same. The strategies are never the same. The outcomes are never the same. It is a game that grows with you.

Can I play competitive games with my partner?

Yes. But you must accept that the game will feel different. You must accept that the strategy will shift from optimization to obstruction. You must accept that the fun will come from the competition, not the collaboration. Choose accordingly.

Here is the math that separates players who win from players who grab randomly.

]]> In 2016, a designer at Ystari Games ran a playtest where the first three players all grabbed the exact same three starting gems. The game didn’t break. It didn’t stall. It just revealed a hidden truth about Splendor: the opening gem grab isn’t about what you take. It’s about what you refuse to take.

Splendor teaches one specific lesson that every gateway game should, and almost none of them do: the first move decides the game before the first turn ends. This isn’t about luck. It’s about reading the board’s silent signals and making the hard choice to leave a gem behind.

Most new players treat the opening round like a treasure hunt. They scan the board, grab the most abundant gem, and move on. This is exactly why they lose. The board is lying to you. The most common gem is the most dangerous gem to take first.

Here is the math that separates players who win from players who grab randomly.

The Gem Distribution Trap

Splendor uses a fixed gem distribution: 20 white, 15 blue, 10 red, 5 green, and 4 black. Most players see 20 white and think ‘safe.’ They are wrong. White is the most expensive gem to hold. It sits in your hand, doing nothing, while your opponents build engines that convert blue and red into cards.

When you grab white in round one, you are betting that white will remain abundant. It won’t. Every white gem taken by every player reduces the supply. By round three, white is often the scarcest gem on the board, and you are holding a hand full of it. You have to sell it back for half value, or worse, you are stuck with it while your opponent grabs the last blue gem and buys a level two card.

The fix is simple but counterintuitive: grab the rarest gem available on the board in round one. If there are only four black gems left, take one. If there are five green, take one. You are not grabbing a gem you can use. You are grabbing a gem your opponents need. You are forcing them to overpay, and you are keeping your options open for cards that actually matter.

Reading the Card Ranks

Splendor’s card ranks (one, two, and three) are not just points. They are tempo. A level three card costs 7+ gems. A level one card costs 3. The gap is massive. But the real gap is in the gem requirements.

Level three cards demand specific gem combinations. A card requiring 4 blue and 3 red is completely different from a card requiring 2 white, 2 blue, 2 red, and 1 green. The first is a focused engine. The second is a scattered grab. New players chase the scattered cards because they look cheaper. They are wrong.

Focused cards are faster. If you have 4 blue and 3 red, you buy the card. You now have 7 points. Your opponent has 2 white, 2 blue, 2 red, and 1 green. They buy the scattered card. They now have 7 points. But your opponent’s hand is now full of white and green. They have to sell. You have no gems to sell. You have tempo. You buy another focused card. They are stuck selling.

The lesson is clear: prioritize cards with fewer gem types, even if the total gem cost is higher. A 7-gem card with 2 gem types is faster than a 6-gem card with 4 gem types. Speed wins the game. Points are just the scoreboard.

The Noble Timing Problem

Splendor’s nobles are the game’s hidden pressure valve. They reward you with a free chip when you meet their gem requirements. Most players treat nobles as afterthoughts. They are not. They are the game’s primary win condition.

The noble that appears first on the board is not the one you should chase. The noble that appears last is. Why? Because nobles are revealed one at a time. The first noble is often easy to complete. The second noble is harder. The third noble is the hardest. But the third noble is also the one that decides the game.

If you complete the first noble, you get a chip. You use that chip to complete the second noble. You get another chip. You use that chip to complete the third noble. You get a third chip. You now have three free chips. You have won the game. This is the noble chain. It is the fastest way to 15 points. It is also the most dangerous path.

The noble chain only works if you control the board’s gem supply. If you grab the wrong gems in round one, you cannot complete the first noble. You cannot start the chain. You are stuck grinding cards while your opponent chains nobles and wins.

The fix is to look at the first noble on the board and ask: can I complete this with the gems I can realistically grab? If the answer is no, ignore it. Chase the second noble. If the second noble is also impossible, chase the third. Do not chase the first noble just because it is first. Chase the noble that fits your opening gem grab.

The Opening Move That Wins

Splendor is not a gem-grabbing game. It is a tempo game disguised as a gem-grabbing game. The opening move decides the game before the first turn ends. Grab the rarest gem available. Ignore the most abundant. Chase focused cards over scattered ones. Chase the noble that fits your opening grab, not the noble that is first on the board.

This is the lesson every gateway game should teach. It is not about luck. It is about reading the board’s silent signals and making the hard choice to leave a gem behind. The player who understands this wins. The player who does not loses. It is that simple.

The next time you play Splendor, watch your opening move. Watch what you grab. Watch what you refuse. Watch what happens in round three. You will see the game decide itself. You will see the opening move win before the first turn ends. You will see why Splendor is the perfect gateway game.
