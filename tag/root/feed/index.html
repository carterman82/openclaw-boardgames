]]> You just finished a six-hour session of Root. The cats are on the roof, the birds are in the trees, and the mice are digging tunnels in the forest. Your group is buzzing, arguing about whether the Vagabond was truly neutral or just secretly rooting for the squirrels. You feel that specific, rare high of playing a game where every player is doing something fundamentally different, yet all of it matters. Then someone asks, “What should we play next?” and you open your shelf, see a dozen symmetric strategy games, and feel that familiar drop in your stomach. You don’t want another game where everyone plays the same rules with different colors. You want that friction again. You want games like Root.

Asymmetry in board games is often confused with cosmetic differences. A game might give Player A a bonus to building and Player B a bonus to combat, but if the underlying decision-making loop is identical for both, the game is symmetric in practice. True asymmetry means the win conditions, resource curves, and core strategies diverge so sharply that what works for one player is actively harmful for another. Root achieved this by making the Marauders play a clock-based race, the Birds play a hand-management engine, and the squirrels play a area-control game, all on the same board. Finding games that replicate this depth of designer-driven complexity is difficult. Most modern games offer slight variations. These three offer entirely different games.

Clank! In the Crypt: The Hidden Game Inside a Card Game

Most players approach Clank! In the Crypt as a standard dungeon-crawler. They see the cards, the dice, and the shared board, and assume they are playing the same game as their opponents. This assumption is exactly why they lose. The game is actually three distinct games played simultaneously, and the asymmetry comes from the specific deck each player builds at the start of the match.

Unlike Root, where asymmetry is baked into the faction rules, Clank! In the Crypt generates asymmetry through deck construction. Each player receives a unique starting deck that dictates their primary strategy. One player might receive a deck heavy on combat cards, forcing them to engage the dungeon’s guardians directly. Another might receive a deck focused on movement and evasion, allowing them to bypass danger entirely. A third might receive a deck optimized for treasure extraction, prioritizing speed over safety.

This creates a dynamic where your opening hand determines your role for the entire game. If you draw a combat-heavy deck, you become the tank, absorbing the dungeon’s aggression so your treasure-focused friends can slip past. If you draw a movement deck, you become the scout, mapping the dungeon and triggering traps for your friends to avoid. The asymmetry is not static; it is generated by the random deal and refined by your card choices. This mirrors Root’s Vagabond, whose role is not defined by a rulebook but by the interactions they choose to engage with.

The strategic depth comes from the hidden information. You do not know your opponent’s starting deck until the first turn. This forces you to adapt your strategy in real-time, much like the Marauders in Root, who must adjust their expansion speed based on the Birds’ engine output. The game rewards players who can read their opponent’s deck composition early and adjust their own strategy to complement or counter it. It is a game of hidden roles and emergent strategy, where the asymmetry is not just thematic, but mechanical.

Twilight Imperium 4th Edition: The Political Asymmetry of Faction Abilities

While Root’s asymmetry is tactical, Twilight Imperium 4th Edition makes it political. This game does not just give you different rules; it gives you a different relationship to the game itself. Each of the twelve factions has a unique ability that fundamentally alters how they interact with the core mechanics of space control, trade, and warfare.

Consider the Embers of Khura. Their faction ability allows them to place ships on the board without spending resources, effectively giving them a permanent, free presence in the game. This changes the entire economic model of the match. Other factions must spend credits to maintain their fleets; the Khura can simply expand. This forces other players to react to the Khura’s presence, altering their trade routes and military strategies. The asymmetry here is not just about what you can do, but how your existence changes the board for everyone else.

Then there is the Naaz-Rokha Alliance. Their ability allows them to move ships through wormholes without spending action points, giving them unparalleled mobility. This changes the strategic value of the galaxy map. Wormholes, which are usually dangerous choke points, become highways for the Naaz-Rokha. This forces other players to rethink their defensive positions and trade routes. The asymmetry is embedded in the geometry of the game itself, creating a unique strategic landscape for each faction.

This level of designer-driven complexity is rare. Most games offer asymmetric factions as minor bonuses. Twilight Imperium 4th Edition makes the faction ability the core of the game. It forces players to learn not just their own strategy, but the strategies of every other faction. This mirrors the political intrigue of Root, where understanding your opponent’s win condition is as important as executing your own. The game rewards players who can adapt to the shifting political landscape, just as the Vagabond in Root must adapt to the shifting power dynamics of the forest.

Small World: The Asymmetry of Combo Building

Small World offers a different kind of asymmetry: the asymmetry of combo building. In this game, each player chooses a race and a special ability at the start of the game. These two choices combine to create a unique strategic identity for each player. The race determines your movement and combat capabilities, while the special ability modifies how you interact with the board.

Consider the Dwarves. They are a race that values defense and resource generation. When paired with the Necromancers special ability, they become a powerful force that can raise the dead to fight for them. This creates a unique strategy focused on attrition and resource management. Now consider the Trolls. They are a race that values strength and aggression. When paired with the Economists special ability, they become a force focused on resource extraction and economic dominance. This creates a completely different strategy, focused on building an engine rather than fighting wars.

The asymmetry in Small World comes from the interaction between the race and the special ability. No two players play the same game, even if they are using the same rules. This creates a dynamic where you must constantly adapt your strategy to your opponent’s choices. If your opponent chooses a race that is strong in combat, you might choose a race that is strong in movement. If your opponent chooses a special ability that focuses on resource generation, you might choose a special ability that focuses on combat.

Small World makes the faction choice the core of the game.

Why These Games Work for Root Fans

Root fans are drawn to the game because it offers a rare combination of thematic depth and mechanical complexity. These three games replicate that combination in different ways. Clank! Twilight Imperium 4th Edition offers political asymmetry through faction abilities. Small World offers strategic asymmetry through combo building. Each game offers a different flavor of asymmetry, but all three offer the same core experience: the feeling that you are playing a unique game, distinct from your opponents, yet deeply connected to them through the shared board.

These games are not just alternatives to Root. They are complements. They offer new ways to experience the same core thrill: the thrill of playing a game where every player is doing something fundamentally different, yet all of it matters. If you are looking for games that deliver the same designer-driven complexity as Root, these are the games to play. They offer the same depth, the same friction, and the same rare high of playing a game where every player is doing something fundamentally different, yet all of it matters.

]]> In 2012, the board game industry was still recovering from the tail end of the Eurogame boom, a period defined by clean mechanics, hidden scoring, and a distinct lack of direct player interaction. Then Cole Wehrle released Root. It was not clean. It was not about hidden scoring. It was about asymmetric factions fighting over a forest, each with their own rules, their own win conditions, and their own relationship to the board. Players did not just play the game; they played a different game than their opponents. Most designers of that era were still trying to make the game more balanced, more streamlined, and more accessible. Wehrle did the opposite. He made it harder, deeper, and more chaotic, and in doing so, he fundamentally altered how modern hobbyists approach game design.

Cole Wehrle is not a prolific designer. He has released fewer than ten major titles in over a decade, yet his influence on the modern board game landscape is disproportionate to his output. His signature is not a single mechanic, but a design philosophy centered on asymmetric faction play, heavy negotiation, and the deliberate creation of friction between players. To understand Wehrle’s work is to understand why modern board games have moved away from symmetric optimization and toward chaotic, narrative-driven conflict. Ranking his games is not merely an exercise in cataloging; it is a map of the evolution of the hobby itself.

Wehrle’s games are defined by their refusal to treat all players equally. In a standard Eurogame, every player starts with the same resources, the same pieces, and the same path to victory. Wehrle’s games start with inequality. This is not a flaw to be balanced out; it is the core engine of the experience. When players have different goals, different tools, and different relationships to the board, the game ceases to be a race to the finish line and becomes a complex web of alliances, betrayals, and shifting power dynamics. This is the Wehrle signature, and it appears in every single one of his major releases, from Root to Scythe to Twilight Imperium 4th Edition.

The Asymmetric Foundation: Root and the Faction Design

Root (2018, Leder Games) is widely considered Wehrle’s masterpiece, and for good reason. It took the asymmetric faction concept and pushed it to its logical extreme. The four main factions, the Vagabond, the Marauders, the Woodland Alliance, and the Birds, do not just have different starting positions. They have different win conditions, different movement rules, and different ways of interacting with the board. The Vagabond, for example, cannot build cities. They can only score points by completing quests and influencing the forest. The Marauders play a clock, trying to score points before the other factions can stop them. The Woodland Alliance builds an engine, slowly grinding out resources and points over time. The Birds control the sky, denying ground access and scoring through air superiority.

This asymmetry creates a game where no two plays are alike. A strategy that works perfectly in one session might be completely useless in another, depending on which factions are present and how they interact. Wehrle designed Root to be difficult to learn, requiring players to read the rulebooks of multiple factions before they can even start playing. But once they do, the depth of interaction is unmatched. Players are not just competing for resources; they are competing for the very rules of the game. This is a level of strategic complexity that few other designers have attempted, and fewer still have executed as well.

The success of Root proved that modern players were willing to accept complexity and asymmetry if the payoff was a richer, more engaging experience. It shifted the industry away from the clean, symmetric optimization of the 2010s and toward the chaotic, narrative-driven conflict that defines much of the current landscape. Wehrle did not invent asymmetric factions, but he perfected them, showing that inequality can be a source of fun rather than frustration.

The Engine Builder: Scythe and the Power of Restraint

If Root is about chaos and interaction, Scythe (2016, Stonemaier Games) is about order and engine building. Released two years before Root, Scythe was Wehrle’s attempt to create a more accessible, yet still deeply strategic, game. It features asymmetric factions, but they are less extreme than those in Root. Each faction has a unique starting unit and a unique ability, but they all share the same basic mechanics: resource gathering, territory control, and engine building.

Scythe is often described as a game about “power and restraint.” Players must decide when to expand, when to fight, and when to consolidate their position. The game features a unique action selection system, where players take turns choosing from a limited pool of actions, forcing them to adapt to their opponents’ moves. This creates a tense, back-and-forth dynamic that is very different from the free-form chaos of Root.

While Scythe is less asymmetric than Root, it is still a Wehrle game in its core philosophy. It prioritizes player interaction and strategic depth over pure optimization. The game is not about building the biggest engine; it is about building the right engine for the situation. This focus on adaptation and flexibility is a hallmark of Wehrle’s design, and it makes Scythe a favorite among players who prefer a more structured, yet still highly interactive, experience.

The Grand Strategy: Twilight Imperium 4th Edition

Before Root and Scythe, Wehrle was best known for Twilight Imperium 4th Edition (2017, Fantasy Flight Games). This is a massive, 6-to-8 player game that spans generations of spacefaring civilizations. It is not an easy game to learn, and it is not a game for everyone. But for those who love it, it is the ultimate expression of Wehrle’s design philosophy.

Twilight Imperium is a game about negotiation, diplomacy, and war. Players form alliances, break them, fight over territory, and try to score victory points through a variety of means. The game features asymmetric factions, each with their own unique abilities and playstyles. It also features a complex political system, where players can propose laws, form coalitions, and manipulate the game’s rules to their advantage.

What makes Twilight Imperium uniquely Wehrle is its focus on the social contract. The game is not just about winning; it is about the story you create with your opponents. The alliances you form, the betrayals you execute, and the wars you fight are all part of the experience. Wehrle designed the game to be a social event, where the interactions between players are just as important as the mechanics on the board. This is a level of social engagement that few other games achieve, and it makes Twilight Imperium a unique and unforgettable experience.

The Early Work: Dominion and the Deck-Building Pioneer

Before his fame as an asymmetric faction designer, Wehrle was a key contributor to Dominion (2008, Rio Grande Games), the game that started the deck-building genre. While he was not the sole designer, his influence on the game’s core mechanics is undeniable. Wehrle’s work on Dominion focused on the concept of deck construction, where players start with a small, weak deck and gradually build it into a powerful engine over the course of the game.

This concept of “deck building” as a core mechanic was revolutionary at the time. It shifted the focus from hand management to long-term strategy, and it influenced a generation of designers who followed. Wehrle’s work on Dominion laid the groundwork for his later asymmetric designs, showing his early interest in creating games where players have different paths to victory and different ways of interacting with the game state.

The Minor Titles: Wyrmspan and Beyond

Wehrle has also released several smaller, less well-known games, such as Wyrmspan (2023, Leder Games). Wyrmspan is a game about dragon breeding, where players raise and breed dragons to score points. The game is more about resource management and engine building, with a heavy focus on the interaction between different dragon types.

While Wyrmspan is not as well-known as Root or Scythe, it is still a Wehrle game in its core philosophy.

Why Wehrle’s Games Matter

Cole Wehrle’s games are not just about winning. They are about the experience of playing. They are about the interactions between players, the stories they create, and the memories they make. Wehrle’s games are difficult to learn, but they are rewarding to master. They require players to think differently, to adapt to their opponents, and to embrace the chaos of the game.

Ranking Wehrle’s games is not just about comparing mechanics or player counts. It is about understanding the evolution of his design philosophy and the impact he has had on the hobby. From Dominion to Root, Wehrle has consistently pushed the boundaries of what board games can be. He has shown that inequality can be a source of fun, that complexity can be engaging, and that social interaction is just as important as the mechanics on the board.

For players who love deep, strategic, and interactive games, Cole Wehrle’s work is essential. His games are not for everyone, but for those who love them, they are unforgettable. They are the games that stay with you long after the box is packed away, the games that spark conversations and debates, and the games that remind you why you fell in love with board games in the first place.

FAQ

Is Cole Wehrle still actively designing games?
Yes, though he releases titles infrequently. His most recent major release is Wyrmspan (2023), and he continues to work on new projects, though he has not announced a timeline for his next major design.

Which Cole Wehrle game is best for beginners?
Scythe is generally considered the most accessible of his major titles. It features asymmetric factions, but they are less extreme than those in Root, and the game has a more structured action selection system that is easier to learn.

Does Wehrle only design asymmetric games?
No, but asymmetry is his signature. Dominion was symmetric, and Scythe has mild asymmetry. However, his most celebrated works, Root and Twilight Imperium, feature heavy asymmetry as a core design pillar.

What is the difference between Root and Scythe?
Root is highly asymmetric, with factions having different win conditions and rules. Scythe has mild asymmetry, with factions sharing the same basic mechanics but having unique starting units and abilities. Root is more chaotic and interactive, while Scythe is more structured and engine-focused.

Are Cole Wehrle games suitable for solo play?
Most of his games are designed for multiplayer, but Root and Scythe have excellent solo modes. Root‘s solo mode is particularly well-regarded, offering a challenging and unique experience that differs significantly from the multiplayer game.

]]> Area control games are not about conquering territory. They are about denial. You do not win by building the strongest army in the center of the map; you win by making sure your opponent cannot build anything at all. This is the single most common failure mode in modern strategy gaming, and it is the reason half the players at your table spend three hours building massive armies that never get to move.

When you play a game like Twilight Imperium, Root, or Concordia, your instinct is to look at the board and see space. You see empty hexes, unclaimed regions, and unsecured borders. You treat those empty spaces as resources to be claimed, much like you would treat wood in Carcassonne or cards in Dominion. But space in an area control game is not a resource. It is a liability. Every piece you place on the board is a target. Every hex you hold is a debt you must pay every single turn to keep.

This distinction between claiming and holding is what separates the players who feel like they are playing a strategy game from the players who feel like they are playing a target practice simulator. The former understand that the primary strategic action in area control is not expansion, but restriction. You are not trying to fill the board. You are trying to shrink it.

The Cost of Presence

Let us look at the basic math of area control. In almost every modern area control game, holding a region requires you to spend resources to defend it. In Root, the Woodland League must spend cards to recruit new units when the map shifts. In Concordia, you must spend coins to move your workers off a region to avoid penalties. In Twilight Imperium, you must spend action cards and fleet points to maintain your borders against enemy incursions.

Every region you hold is a drain on your economy. This is the core design tension that every area control game is built around. If holding territory were free, the game would be solved instantly: the first player to act would claim every hex, and the game would end. The fun, and the strategy, comes from the fact that holding territory is expensive.

Most players ignore this cost. They see an empty region, they see a strong army, and they move. They do not calculate the maintenance cost of that army. They do not calculate the opportunity cost of not spending those resources on their own engine. They treat the board as a static map, when it is actually a dynamic economy of force projection.

Consider the opening phase of Root. The Marauders do not win by building the largest army. They win by controlling the flow of the game. They use their movement to threaten regions, forcing their opponents to spend resources on defense. The Marauders do not need to hold the center of the map. They need to make sure their opponents cannot build there. This is denial. This is area control done right.

The Illusion of Expansion

Expansion is the most seductive trap in area control. It feels good to place a piece on a new hex. It feels like progress. It feels like you are winning. But expansion is only valid if it increases your net power. If you expand into a region that costs you more to hold than it generates, you are losing power. You are borrowing strength from your future self to pay for your present ego.

This is why the best area control players rarely expand until they have secured their core. They build a small, dense, unbreakable core of control. They do not spread out. They do not try to be everywhere. They try to be nowhere their opponents can reach. They use their pieces to block, to threaten, and to deny. They do not use their pieces to conquer.

Look at Concordia. The game is played on a small board. There is very little room to expand. The strategy is not about taking more space. It is about taking the right space. It is about positioning your workers so that your opponents cannot use the regions they need. It is about denying them the ability to execute their turns. It is about making their expansion impossible, not by fighting them, but by being there first.

The Psychology of Denial

Area control is not just a mechanical game. It is a psychological one. When you play a game like Root, you are not just fighting your opponent’s armies. You are fighting their plans. You are trying to make their plans impossible. You are trying to force them to play a different game than the one they brought to the table.

This is why denial is so powerful. It is not just about stopping your opponent from scoring points. It is about stopping them from having fun. It is about making them feel powerless, frustrated, and reactive. It is about making them play your game, not theirs.

When you play a game like Twilight Imperium, you are not just fighting your opponent’s fleets. You are fighting their political alliances. You are trying to isolate them, to cut off their resources, to make them feel alone. It is a game of pressure, of squeezing the life out of your opponents until they have no choice but to fold.

When Expansion Is the Right Move

Denial is not always the right move. There are times when expansion is necessary. There are times when you must take space to secure your future. There are times when you must expand to build your engine. But these times are rare. They are the exception, not the rule.

The key is to understand when expansion is valid. Expansion is valid when it secures your core. Expansion is valid when it forces your opponents to spend more than they gain. Expansion is valid when it is a calculated risk, not a reckless gamble.

If you can master this distinction, if you can learn to play the game of denial instead of the game of conquest, you will win more games. You will win games you should lose. You will win games that feel impossible. You will win games that your opponents will never understand.

It is about denial. It is about making sure your opponents cannot play their game. It is about making sure they cannot build their armies. It is about making sure they cannot score their points. It is about making sure they cannot win.

Stop trying to conquer the board. Start trying to shrink it. Start trying to make your opponents feel powerless. Start trying to make them play your game. Start trying to win.

The Marauders: The Game Ends When You Decide It Ends

The Marauders are the only faction in Root that does not need to build an engine to win. They do not need to draw cards. They do not need to build structures. They do not need to control the forest. They need to capture villages. That is it. If you capture four villages, you win. If you capture three villages and the game ends, you lose. The math is brutally simple.

The problem is that the other three factions are building engines while the Marauders are capturing villages. The Woodworkers are placing wood to build houses. The Birds are placing cards to fly. The Cats are placing cats to control the forest. The Marauders are capturing villages to win. The Marauders are not playing a resource game. They are playing a clock.

The Marauders must play aggressively from turn one. They cannot afford to build houses. They cannot afford to place workers. They cannot afford to build engines. They must capture villages. They must capture villages quickly. They must capture villages before the other factions build their engines. They must capture villages before the other factions realize what is happening.

This is why the Marauders are so often misunderstood. Players look at their deck and see a small deck with no card draw and no engine. They think they need to build. They think they need to be patient. They think they need to play the other factions. They are wrong. And the clock is ticking.

The opening move in Root is not about resources. It is about timing. It is about deciding what game you are playing. It is about deciding when to strike. It is about deciding when to stop building and start attacking.

If you play the Marauders, your opening move is to capture a village. If you play the Woodworkers, your opening move is to place wood. If you play the Birds, your opening move is to place a card.

These are not four variations of the same game. These are four different games. These are four different relationships with time. These are four different strategies for winning. These are four different ways to lose.

The opening move decides the game because it decides what game you are playing. If you play the Marauders and you build, you lose. If you play the Woodworkers and you capture, you lose.

The opening move decides the game because it decides when to strike. If you play the Birds and you strike, you lose. If you play the Cats and you wait, you lose.

The opening move decides the game because it decides when to stop building and start attacking.

Conclusion: Play the Clock, Not the Cards

Root is not a game about cards. It is a game about time. It is a game about when to strike. It is a game about when to stop playing one game and start playing another.

It decides when to strike.

Stop treating Root as a deck-building game. Stop treating it as a worker-placement game. Stop treating it as a resource-management game. Stop treating it as a game about cards.
