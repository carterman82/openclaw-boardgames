]]> You will lose Expeditions if you play it correctly.

Every strategy guide, every forum post, and every video essay will tell you to hoard gold, hoard cards, and hoard your actions. They will tell you to treat the campaign like a spreadsheet and the board like a ledger. If you follow that advice, you will lose. You will lose because Expeditions is not a resource-management game disguised as a campaign. It is a risk-assessment engine that punishes caution and rewards the specific, calculated aggression of a player who understands that the game is not about building wealth. It is about surviving the pressure of time.

Most players approach Expeditions with the mindset of a banker. They see the gold tracks, the card upgrades, and the action points, and they assume the goal is to maximize efficiency. They sit on their hand, waiting for the perfect moment to play a high-value card. They save their gold for the endgame, assuming that a massive payout will secure victory. This is the standard solo playbook. It is also the reason you are losing.

Expeditions, designed by Wolfgang Warsch and published by Lookout Games in 2023, is a solo-only campaign game that simulates the logistics of 19th-century exploration. You are not just playing a game; you are managing a multi-year campaign across a modular board that changes every session. The game uses a deck of cards to represent your team, your equipment, and the challenges you face. But the core mechanic is not the cards. It is the tension between your current resources and the escalating threats of the campaign.

When you play Expeditions the “right” way, you are playing a game of accumulation. You are trying to build the strongest possible engine before the final scenario hits. But the game is designed to break that engine. The campaign does not wait for you to be ready. It advances. The threats grow. The board expands. And if you are sitting on a pile of gold and a hand of powerful cards, you are not safe. You are a target. The game forces you to spend what you have, when you have it, because holding it becomes a liability.

The Hoarding Trap: Why Efficiency Is a Lie

The primary failure mode in Expeditions is the hoarding trap. This is not a minor strategic error; it is a fundamental misunderstanding of the game’s core loop. When you hold gold, you are not saving it for a rainy day. You are paying interest on your own failure. Every turn you do not spend gold, you are missing an opportunity to upgrade your team, to buy better cards, or to secure a critical action. The game does not penalize you for spending gold directly, but it penalizes you for the opportunity cost of not spending it.

Consider the card economy. In Expeditions, cards are not just points. They are actions. They are resources. They are your ability to interact with the board. When you hold a high-value card in your hand, you are not saving a point. You are withholding an action. You are refusing to engage with the game. The game will not wait for you to play that card. The scenarios will advance. The threats will escalate. And when they do, you will be forced to play that card under duress, or not at all.

This is why the standard advice to “hoard gold and cards” is so dangerous. It assumes that the game is static. It assumes that the board will stay the same, that the threats will remain constant, and that you will have time to build your perfect engine. But Expeditions is a dynamic system. The board changes. The scenarios change. The threats change. And if you are not adapting, you are falling behind. The game punishes stagnation. It rewards movement. It rewards the player who is willing to spend, to risk, and to fail.

The Aggression Paradox: Why You Must Spend to Win

The second key to winning Expeditions is understanding the aggression paradox. Most players believe that aggression means attacking. In Expeditions, aggression means spending. It means playing cards. It means taking actions. It means engaging with the board. The player who spends the most, wins the most. Not because they are lucky, but because they are forcing the game to react to them.

When you play a card, you are not just resolving an effect. You are changing the state of the game. You are forcing the scenario to advance. You are forcing the threats to escalate. You are forcing the board to change. And in doing so, you are controlling the pace of the game. The player who controls the pace controls the game. The player who hoards is controlled by the game.

This is why the standard advice to “save your gold for the endgame” is so wrong. The endgame does not care about your gold. The endgame cares about your actions. The endgame cares about your cards. The endgame cares about your ability to adapt. If you are sitting on a pile of gold and a hand of cards, you are not ready for the endgame. You are ready for a loss. The endgame will test your ability to spend, to risk, and to fail. And if you have not practiced that, you will fail.

The Campaign Structure: Why Every Session Matters

Expeditions is a campaign game. This means that every session matters. Every decision you make in Session 1 affects Session 2. Every card you play in Session 2 affects Session 3. Every gold you spend in Session 3 affects Session 4. The game is not a series of isolated puzzles. It is a continuous narrative. And the narrative is written by your actions.

Most players treat campaign games like a series of standalone puzzles. They solve Session 1, then Session 2, then Session 3, without considering how one session affects the next. But in Expeditions, the sessions are linked. The board is linked. The cards are linked. The gold is linked. And if you are not thinking about the long game, you are losing the short game.

This is why the standard advice to “focus on the current scenario” is so dangerous. The current scenario is not the only scenario. The current scenario is just the first step in a longer journey. And if you are not planning for that journey, you are not playing the game. You are just playing the scenario.

The Failure Modes: When Hoarding Becomes Fatal

There are specific moments in Expeditions where hoarding becomes fatal. These are not rare edge cases. They are common failure points that every player will encounter. The first is the “gold cap” scenario. In some scenarios, there is a limit to how much gold you can hold. If you exceed that limit, you lose points. If you are hoarding gold, you are likely to hit that cap. And if you hit that cap, you lose points. It is that simple.

The second is the “card exhaustion” scenario. In some scenarios, you are forced to play cards from your hand. If you are holding high-value cards, you are forced to play them. And if you are forced to play them, you are forced to play them under duress. And if you are forced to play them under duress, you are likely to play them wrong. And if you play them wrong, you lose.

The third is the “time pressure” scenario. In some scenarios, you are running out of time. You are running out of actions. You are running out of turns. And if you are hoarding gold and cards, you are not ready for the time pressure. You are not ready for the rush. You are not ready for the chaos. And if you are not ready for the chaos, you will lose.

The Winning Strategy: Play It Wrong to Win

So how do you win Expeditions? You play it wrong. You spend your gold. You play your cards. You take your actions. You engage with the board. You force the game to react to you. You control the pace. You adapt to the changes. You fail when you need to fail. And you win when you need to win.

This is not a strategy. It is a mindset. It is a way of thinking about the game that prioritizes action over accumulation, engagement over hoarding, and adaptation over planning. It is a way of playing that accepts failure as part of the process, and that sees risk as an opportunity, not a threat.

If you can adopt this mindset, you will win Expeditions. Not because you are smarter than the other players. Not because you are luckier. Not because you have better cards. But because you are playing the game correctly. You are playing it wrong. And in Expeditions, that is the only way to win.

The game is not about building wealth. It is about surviving the pressure. It is about spending them. It is about engaging with the present. And if you can let go of the need to control every variable, you will find that the game controls itself. You will find that the game is not your enemy. It is your partner. And together, you will win.

]]> Anime board games are not about collecting miniatures. They are about the rulebook. You can buy a display case full of 28mm resin figures of Goku, Naruto, or Sailor Moon, but none of them will tell you how to resolve a combat interaction, how the victory points are calculated, or why your opponent’s ‘special move’ card overrides your defense. The miniatures are the decoration. The rulebook is the game.

This is the single most common failure mode for players approaching the anime board game genre. You walk into a hobby shop, see a beautifully painted figure of a character you love, and assume the game is a celebration of that character’s power. It isn’t. It is a tightly tuned, often highly asymmetric, strategy game that happens to wear a pop-culture skin. Treating an anime board game like a static display leads to confusion, broken interactions, and a deeply unsatisfying play experience.

The genre is not a monolith. It spans everything from the cooperative chaos of Forbidden Land to the highly tactical, asymmetric warfare of Attack on Titan: The Board Game or My Hero Academia: The Board Game. But across all of them, the core dynamic remains the same: the rulebook is the primary text, and the miniatures are merely the secondary, aesthetic layer.

The Miniature Trap: Why Paints Don’t Matter

Let’s be clear about the miniatures. They are expensive. They are heavy. They take up shelf space. And for 90% of the gameplay, they do absolutely nothing. They are tokens with extra steps. When a game tells you to move your unit three spaces, it does not matter whether you are moving a plastic figure of a ninja, a resin statue of a magical girl, or a cardboard meeple. The math is identical. The strategic outcome is identical.

The trap lies in the psychological investment. Players spend hours painting these figures. They feel a sense of ownership and attachment to their specific character’s physical representation. When the rules force them to sacrifice their beloved, hand-painted character to achieve a strategic goal, they resist. They hesitate. They question the rules. They feel the game is being ‘unfair’ because it asks them to move their ‘hero’ into a danger zone.

This is a fundamental misunderstanding of the medium. In board games, figures are not characters. They are resources. They are pawns. They are visual aids for spatial tracking. The moment you start treating your painted miniatures as extensions of your emotional investment in the IP, you have lost the game. You are playing a fantasy, not a strategy game.

Compare this to Root, where your units are abstract markers representing a faction’s military presence. No one mourns moving a cat soldier into a forest tile. The abstraction is clean. The rules are clear. The emotional attachment to the ‘cat’ is zero. Anime games try to bridge this gap by giving you a physical anchor for your strategic decisions. It is a beautiful idea. It is also a strategic liability if you let it cloud your judgment.

The Rulebook Is the Only Truth

When a rulebook and a miniature conflict, the rulebook wins. Always. This is the golden rule of the genre. If a miniature’s base says ‘move 4 spaces’ but the card says ‘move 2 spaces,’ the card wins. If a character’s pose implies they are holding a sword, but the rules say they are using a bow, the bow wins. If a figure looks like it should be immune to fire, but the rulebook says it takes double damage, it takes double damage.

This conflict is not a design flaw. It is a design feature. The miniatures are designed to be visually appealing, often capturing a character’s most dynamic pose. That pose is rarely mechanically accurate. A character mid-leap looks fast. A character crouching looks defensive. A character with a giant sword looks powerful. None of these visual cues are reliable. The text on the cards and the text in the rulebook are the only reliable sources of information.

Consider Attack on Titan: The Board Game. The miniatures of the Survey Corps are incredibly detailed, capturing the tension and scale of the Titans. But the game’s core mechanic is the ‘omn-directional gear’ system, which is purely abstract. The miniatures do not move using the gear. They move according to a complex set of rules governing movement, engagement, and titan interaction. If you try to move your miniature based on how the gear looks, you will break the game. If you follow the rulebook, you will win.

Consider My Hero Academia: The Board Game. The miniatures of the students are vibrant and colorful. The miniatures do not have special abilities. The cards do. If you try to use your miniature’s ‘pose’ to justify a special ability, you will break the game.

Asymmetry Is the Heart of the Genre

Most modern anime board games are asymmetric. This means each player is playing a different game. This is not a bug. It is the core design philosophy. In a symmetric game, every player has the same tools, the same goals, and the same win conditions. In an asymmetric game, every player has different tools, different goals, and different win conditions.

Asymmetry is the primary reason you play these games. It is the primary reason the IP is licensed. It is the primary reason the miniatures are different. If every player had the same miniatures, the game would be symmetric. If the game were symmetric, it would be boring. Asymmetry creates tension. It creates conflict. It creates the ‘anime’ experience.

But asymmetry also creates complexity. It creates edge cases. It creates situations where the rulebook is the only thing that can resolve the conflict. When Player A’s character has a ‘stealth’ ability and Player B’s character has a ‘detect’ ability, the rulebook dictates the interaction. The miniatures do not. The miniatures do not have ‘stealth’ or ‘detect’. The cards do. The rulebook does.

This is why the rulebook is the only truth. It is the only thing that can resolve the conflict. It is the only thing that can define the game. Without the rulebook, the miniatures are just plastic. Without the rulebook, the cards are just paper. Without the rulebook, the game is just a collection of toys.

How to Play the Game, Not the Toy

So how do you play an anime board game correctly? How do you separate the toy from the game? How do you focus on the rulebook and ignore the miniatures? Here is a simple framework.

1. Read the Rulebook First. Before you touch a single miniature, read the rulebook. Read it twice. Read it a third time if you are confused. The rulebook is the source of truth. The miniatures are the source of decoration. Do not confuse the two.

2. Ignore the Miniatures. When you are making a strategic decision, ignore the miniatures. Do not look at the pose. Do not look at the paint. Do not look at the detail. Look at the cards. Look at the board. Look at the rules. The miniatures are irrelevant. They are just visual aids. They do not affect the strategy. They do not affect the outcome. They do not affect the game.

3. Trust the Cards. When a card says ‘move 4 spaces,’ move 4 spaces. When a card says ‘deal 2 damage,’ deal 2 damage. When a card says ‘ignore this effect,’ ignore this effect. The cards are the source of truth.

4. Embrace the Asymmetry. If you are playing an asymmetric game, embrace the asymmetry. Play your character’s abilities. Play your character’s goals. Play your character’s win conditions. Do not try to play your opponent’s game. Do not try to play the game your opponent is playing. Play your game. Play your character. Play the rulebook.

5. Paint the Miniatures Last. If you must paint the miniatures, paint them last. Paint them after you have played the game. Paint them after you have read the rulebook. Paint them after you have understood the strategy. If you paint them first, you will be disappointed. If you paint them last, you will be satisfied.

The Real Value Is in the Strategy

The real value of an anime board game is not in the miniatures. It is in the strategy. It is in the rulebook. It is in the interaction between the players. It is in the tension of the game.

When you play an anime board game correctly, you are not playing with miniatures. You are playing with rules. You are playing with cards. You are playing with strategy. You are playing with other players. You are playing the game.

The miniatures are just a bonus. They are just a decoration. They are just a way to make the game look nice. They are not the game. The strategy is the game. The interaction is the game. The players are the game.

Stop collecting miniatures. Start reading rulebooks. Stop painting figures. Start playing games. Stop chasing IP. Start chasing strategy. The miniatures are not the point. The rulebook is the point. The strategy is the point. The game is the point.

When you understand this, you will stop buying miniatures. You will start buying games. You will stop collecting figures. You will start collecting experiences. You will stop chasing IP. You will start chasing strategy.

]]> Most players spend their first dozen games of Ticket to Ride building a beautiful, useless network. They grab colorful plastic trains, connect cities, and feel productive. Then they look at their score sheet and realize they spent forty minutes connecting nothing they were paid to connect. The game ends, they lose, and they blame the dice or their opponent’s “aggressive” blocking. They are not losing because of bad luck. They are losing because they treated a spatial puzzle like a construction project.

The core error in Ticket to Ride is the assumption that the board state is the game. It is not. The board state is just the canvas. The game is the hidden contract between your hand of destination tickets and the physical space you claim. When you play Ticket to Ride, you are not trying to build the most connected network. You are trying to fulfill specific, often conflicting, spatial obligations while preventing your opponents from fulfilling theirs. The route that wins before the board is set is the one you plan in your head during the first three turns, before you place a single train car.

The Ticket Paradox: Why Connectivity Is a Trap

Every new player approaches Ticket to Ride with a logical instinct: build a dense, interconnected network. If you have trains in Chicago, you should connect them to St. Louis, then to New Orleans, then to Atlanta. This creates a robust web. It looks good. It feels safe. But in Ticket to Ride, a dense network is often a liability. Why? Because dense networks are expensive to maintain and easy to block.

Consider the classic cross-country route: Seattle to New York. It requires roughly 18 trains. If you build a dense web across the Midwest, you might spend 30 trains to connect ten cities. You have spent 12 extra trains on “connectivity” that yields zero points. Those 12 trains cost you 60 coins. In a game where the average score is 110 to 130 points, you have just set yourself back 15% of your potential score before you even drew your next ticket.

The winning strategy ignores connectivity. It embraces sparsity. The best routes in Ticket to Ride are long, direct, and often single-threaded. They do not branch. They do not loop. They go from point A to point B with the minimum number of trains required. This is counter-intuitive because we are trained to value redundancy. In real life, redundant roads are good. In Ticket to Ride, redundancy is bankruptcy. The player who wins is the one who builds the fewest trains to fulfill the most tickets.

This is why the opening ticket grab is so critical. Most players grab tickets that are close together, or tickets that fit into a natural geographic cluster. This is safe, but it is also low-yield. The high-yield tickets are the ones that span the map: Vancouver to Halifax, Seattle to New York, Denver to Atlanta. These tickets are hard to complete. They require crossing hostile territory. They require anticipating where your opponents will block. But when completed, they are worth 10, 12, or even 18 points. A single completed cross-country ticket is worth more than five small regional connections. The strategy is not to build more. It is to build less, but build it where it counts.

Reading the Map: The First Three Turns Decide the Game

The game is won or lost in the first three turns. This is not hyperbole. By turn three, the board has a specific topology. Certain routes are still open. Certain cities are still accessible. Once a route is blocked, the cost to bypass it increases exponentially. This is the concept of “blocking cost.” If you want to build a route through Chicago, and your opponent has already placed two trains there, you now have to go around. Going around might require 4 extra trains. Those 4 trains cost 20 coins. That is the price of being late to the table.

So, what do you do in the first three turns? You do not place trains on the board. You place tickets. You look at your hand of destination tickets and you identify the “anchor” routes. An anchor route is a long, high-value ticket that is still physically possible to complete given the current board state. If you see a ticket that goes from Montreal to New Orleans, and the path through Pittsburgh and St. Louis is still open, that is your anchor. You hold that ticket. You do not try to complete it immediately. You use it as a compass.

While you hold that ticket, you build the infrastructure that supports it. You might not build the full route. You might build the first four trains into Pittsburgh. You might build the last four trains out of New Orleans. You are not trying to connect them yet. You are trying to claim the endpoints. This forces your opponents to react. If they try to block you, they have to spend trains on a route that does not yet exist. If they ignore you, you will complete it. This is the “threat of completion.” It is more powerful than completion itself.

Most players fail to use this threat. They rush to connect cities because they feel the pressure of the timer or the boredom of waiting. But the player who wins is the one who sits on their trains. They wait. They watch. They let their opponents overextend. When an opponent builds a weak, fragmented network, they are vulnerable. They have spent trains on low-value connections. They have no long routes. They have no points. And now, you can swoop in and complete your anchor route, stealing the points they should have had.

The Blocking Game: How to Deny Without Committing

Blocking is the most misunderstood mechanic in Ticket to Ride. Most players think blocking means placing a train on a route to stop someone else. This is true, but it is also extremely expensive. If you place a train to block, you have spent resources. You have not gained points. You have not advanced your own game. You have only hindered another player’s. This is a net loss unless the other player was about to complete a route that would have won the game.

The winning blocker does not place trains on the board. They place tickets in their hand. By holding a high-value ticket that requires a specific route, they create a “phantom threat.” If you see your opponent holding a ticket that goes from Seattle to New York, you know they will eventually try to build that route. You do not need to block it immediately. You just need to make it expensive for them to do so. You can build a short, cheap route that intersects their path. You can claim a city that is a key hub. You do not need to stop them. You just need to make them pay more than they are worth.

This is the “cost of denial.” If your opponent wants to build a route through Chicago, and you have already claimed the route into Chicago, they have to go around. Going around costs them trains. Trains cost coins. Coins cost tickets. Tickets cost turns. By the time they complete their route, they have spent three extra turns and 15 extra coins. That is a net loss of 30 points. If their route was only worth 12 points, they have just lost the game by trying to complete it. This is why blocking is not about stopping people. It is about making their success too expensive to justify.

The key is to block lightly. Do not spend 4 trains to block a 12-point route. Spend 1 or 2 trains to make them go around. Make them pay the “detour tax.” This is the difference between a good player and a great player. A good player blocks aggressively. A great player blocks economically.

Endgame Timing: When to Scoop and When to Stay

The endgame in Ticket to Ride is not triggered by a timer. It is triggered by a player declaring “I am done.” This is a strategic decision that most players make too early. They see they have a few points, they see the board is getting crowded, and they declare “I am done.” This is a mistake. The endgame is not about having the most points. It is about having the most points relative to your opponents’ remaining potential.

When you declare “I am done,” you reveal your hand of destination tickets. If you have uncompleted tickets, you lose points. These points are subtracted from your total. This is the “penalty of exposure.” If you have a ticket that goes from Vancouver to Halifax, and you declare “I am done” before completing it, you lose 10 points. That is a catastrophic loss. It can turn a winning game into a losing one.

So, when should you declare “I am done”? You should declare it when you have completed all your tickets, or when the penalty of completing your remaining tickets is less than the points your opponents will gain by continuing to play. This is the “endgame threshold.” If you have 100 points, and your opponent has 90 points, and your opponent has one ticket left that is worth 12 points, you should not declare “I am done” unless you are sure they cannot complete it. If they can complete it, they will have 102 points. You will have 100. You will lose.

The winning endgame player does not rush. They wait. They watch their opponents. They let their opponents make mistakes. And when they see that their opponents are vulnerable, they declare “I am done.” This is the “scoop.” It is a strategic withdrawal. It is a calculation. It is the moment when the game shifts from a spatial puzzle to a mathematical one.

This is why the first three turns are so important. If you have built a sparse, efficient network, you will have more trains left in your hand. You will have more coins left in your bank. You will have more options. You will have more flexibility. You will be able to wait. You will be able to scoop. And you will win.

The Real Game: Spatial Awareness Over Construction

Ticket to Ride is often criticized for being too simple, for being too luck-based, for being too easy to learn. These criticisms miss the point. Ticket to Ride is not a game about complexity. It is a game about clarity. It is a game about seeing the map clearly, understanding the value of space, and making decisions based on that understanding. It is a game about spatial awareness.

Most players play Ticket to Ride as a construction game. They focus on the trains, the colors, the board state. They ignore the tickets. They ignore the points. They ignore the endgame. They build beautiful, useless networks. They lose. The player who wins is the one who plays Ticket to Ride as a spatial puzzle. They focus on the tickets, the points, the endgame. They ignore the trains. They ignore the colors. They ignore the board state. They build sparse, efficient networks. They win.

This is the lesson of Ticket to Ride. It is about building less. It is about connecting right. It is about playing the tickets. It is about understanding that the route that wins before the board is set is the one you plan in your head, before you place a single train car. It is about realizing that the game is not about the trains. It is about the space between them.

When you sit down to play Ticket to Ride, do not grab the trains. Grab the tickets. Look at the map. Find the anchor routes. Build the infrastructure. Block lightly. Wait for the endgame. Scoop when the math works. And remember: the game is not about the trains. It is about the space. It is about the route that wins before the board is set.

The 24-Hour Velocity Window

Is deck building the same as deck construction?
No. Deck construction happens before the game starts. You build your starting deck based on the rules. Deck building happens during the game. You buy cards from a central market and add them to your personal deck. The two are related but distinct. Deck building is the dynamic part of the game. Deck construction is the static part.

Should I always try to reduce my deck size?
Not always. Sometimes adding a card is better than removing one. If the card you are adding is powerful enough to justify the extra weight, it might be worth it. But you should always ask yourself: is this card worth the space it takes up? Is it worth the probability cost? If the answer is no, you should look for ways to remove your weaker cards instead.

What is the best deck-building game for beginners?
7 Wonders Duel is often recommended for beginners because it does not use a traditional deck. You select cards from a hand of three, pass the rest, and play them. There is no deck bloat. There is no probability curve. It is pure strategy. If you want a traditional deck builder, Dominion is the classic choice. It teaches the core mechanics of deck building without overwhelming you with options.

How do I know when my deck is too big?
A good rule of thumb is that if you are drawing your powerful cards less than 20% of the time, your deck is probably too big. If your turns feel long and your plays feel weak, your deck is probably too big. If you are buying cards without removing them, your deck is probably too big. Pay attention to your draw patterns. They will tell you everything you need to know.
