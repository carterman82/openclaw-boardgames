A true gateway game does two things simultaneously: it respects the player’s time by keeping the first session under an hour, and it respects their intelligence by offering meaningful choices from turn one. The modern classics that actually hold up today are the ones that walk that razor’s edge, offering enough strategic weight to keep experienced players engaged while remaining accessible enough for a first-time buyer.

Why ‘Best Gateway Board Games 2026’ Is a Moving Target

When you search for the best gateway board games 2026 on Reddit or BGG, you are looking for a snapshot of quality that changes every year. The definition of a gateway game has shifted dramatically over the last decade. In the 1990s, a gateway game was simply one that was shorter and had fewer components than a heavy eurogame. Today, the bar is much higher. Players expect the strategic depth of a mid-weight game with the accessibility of a party game.

The reason this search is so difficult is that publishers often label games as ‘gateway’ as a marketing term rather than a design one. A game might be easy to learn but lack any interesting decisions, making it forgettable. Conversely, a game might be deep but require extensive setup and rule explanations, breaking the flow of a casual night. The true modern classics are those that have survived the test of time, maintaining their popularity not because they are new, but because they solve the fundamental problem of player engagement without sacrificing fun.

The Five Modern Classics That Define the Category

If you are looking for the best gateway board games bgg has to offer, you need to look at the titles that consistently rank in the top 50 for weight and playability. These five games represent the current gold standard for modern gateway design, each excelling in a specific context.

1. Ticket to Ride: The Route That Wins Before the Board Is Set

Published in 2004 by Days of Wonder, Ticket to Ride remains the undisputed king of gateway games. Its core mechanic is deceptively simple: collect train cards to claim railway routes connecting cities across a map. The genius lies in the spatial puzzle it creates. Players must balance the desire to complete long, high-scoring routes with the need to block opponents from connecting their own points. It is easy to learn, quick to play, and offers just enough strategic tension to keep players coming back. It is the perfect entry point for anyone intimidated by modern board gaming.

2. Carcassonne: The Tile-Placement Trap That Ruins New Players

Reiner Knizia’s 2000 masterpiece, Carcassonne, introduced millions of players to the concept of tile placement. The board grows organically as players place tiles, creating cities, roads, and monasteries. The strategic depth comes from the placement of followers (meeples). You must decide whether to claim a feature for points immediately or leave it open for future scoring. The game is famously simple, but the interaction between players is fierce. It teaches spatial reasoning and risk assessment without ever feeling like a math problem.

3. King of Tokyo: Why Your Family Plays It Wrong (And What the Dice Actually Mean)

Richard Garfield’s 2011 design, King of Tokyo, is often mistaken for a pure luck game because of its dice-rolling mechanic. In reality, it is a brilliant exercise in probability management and risk assessment. Players take on the roles of giant monsters battling for control of Tokyo. The game forces players to decide when to attack, when to heal, and when to retreat. The dice are not just random numbers; they are resources to be spent. It is fast, loud, and incredibly satisfying, making it a favorite for family game nights and casual groups alike.

4. Splendor: The Opening Move That Wins Before the First Turn

Published in 2014 by Space Cowboys, Splendor is a game about acquiring gems and developing trade routes. The mechanic is straightforward: collect chips to buy cards that give you permanent discount chips. The strategic depth comes from the timing of your purchases. You must anticipate what your opponents are buying and reserve cards to secure them before someone else does. The game is fast, with no downtime, and offers a satisfying engine-building experience without the complexity of traditional eurogames. It is a perfect example of how simple mechanics can create deep strategic interactions.

5. Azul: Why Your Opening Move Decides the Game (And How to Play It Right)

Michael Kiesling’s 2017 design, Azul, is a pattern-building game that has become a modern classic. Players draft colorful tiles to decorate the walls of a palace. The core mechanic is a draft-and-settle system where you must choose which tiles to take from the factory and which to leave for your opponents. The strategic depth comes from planning your pattern while blocking your opponents from completing theirs. The game is visually stunning, easy to learn, and offers just enough strategic tension to keep players engaged.

How to Choose the Right Gateway Game for Your Group

When you are searching for the best gateway board games reddit communities recommend, the most common advice is to match the game to your group’s preferences. If your group enjoys spatial puzzles, Carcassonne or Kingdomino are excellent choices. If they prefer light strategy and resource management, Splendor or Wingspan might be better. If they enjoy social interaction and negotiation, Catan or 7 Wonders are ideal.

The key is to avoid games that are too simple or too complex. A game that is too simple will feel like a coin flip, while a game that is too complex will feel like a chore. The modern classics listed above strike the perfect balance, offering enough strategic depth to keep players engaged while remaining accessible enough for new players. They are the games that have stood the test of time, maintaining their popularity because they solve the fundamental problem of player engagement without sacrificing fun.

Not necessarily. The best gateway games, like King of Tokyo or 7 Wonders, offer enough strategic depth to keep experienced players engaged. The key is finding a game that challenges you without overwhelming you.

]]> The Games Menu: Designing a Board Game Dinner Party That Doesn’t Fizzle

Stop treating game night like a playlist. A board game dinner party requires a curated menu: a warm-up, a collaborative main course, and a light dessert to ensure every guest leaves smiling.

You are not hosting a game night. You are running a dinner party for strangers who share your hobby. The difference is not in the wine list, it is in the pacing. A standard game night lets the loudest player dominate the first hour and the quietest player check out during the second. A dinner party demands a curated arc where every guest leaves with a story, not a complaint about how long the last game took.

Most hosts fail because they throw together three heavy strategy games and hope the group finds a rhythm. It never works. The rhythm comes from sequencing, not selection. You are designing a menu, and the courses must build on each other. Start with a warm-up that gets people talking, move to a collaborative main course that forces interaction, and finish with a light dessert that leaves everyone smiling. If you serve a three-hour engine builder as your second course, you have already failed.

The goal of a board game dinner party is not to crown a winner. It is to create a shared experience that makes your guests want to come back. This requires treating the games as functional tools for social engineering rather than competitive arenas. Your job is to curate the flow, not the high scores.

Course One: The Warm-Up Appetizer

Your first game must achieve three things simultaneously. It must get everyone to the table, it must establish a baseline of communication, and it must finish in under forty-five minutes. If the game takes longer than that, you risk the “first game hangover” where players are too mentally exhausted to commit to the main event.

Avoid any game with a steep learning curve. If you spend the first twenty minutes explaining rules, you have lost the room. Instead, pick a game with intuitive mechanics and high player interaction. Codenames is the gold standard here because it forces teams to talk, but it is also a known quantity for many groups. A better alternative for a true dinner party is a cooperative game where the group wins or loses together. Pandemic Legacy Season 1 is too heavy for an opener, but a standalone cooperative game like Forbidden Island or Castle Panic works perfectly. These games create immediate bonding because the group shares a common enemy. There is no backstabbing, no hidden scoring, and no reason to feel awkward about losing.

The key is to keep the stakes low. If someone makes a mistake in the warm-up, it is funny, not fatal. This sets the tone for the entire evening: you are here to play together, not to destroy each other.

Course Two: The Collaborative Main Course

This is the heart of your dinner party. The main course should be the longest game on the menu, but it must prioritize interaction over optimization. This is where you move away from pure strategy and toward social deduction or negotiation. The best games for this slot are those where the mechanics force players to look at each other, not just the board.

Consider a game like Secret Hitler or The Resistance: Avalon. These are social deduction games that thrive on reading body language and managing lies. They require constant verbal engagement, which keeps the energy high. If your group prefers a more thematic experience, Root is a fantastic choice, but only if you are playing with a group that already understands asymmetric factions. For a mixed group, Ark Nova is too complex for a dinner party setting, but a game like Wingspan can work if you simplify the scoring. However, the true winner for a main course is a game that forces negotiation. Catan is the classic example, but its trading phase often fails, leaving players frustrated and silent. Modern games like Brass: Birmingham are too heavy, but 7 Wonders offers a perfect middle ground. The drafting mechanic forces you to watch your neighbors, and the community buildings phase allows for direct interaction without the chaos of open trading.

The goal here is to create a “shared failure” state. As noted in the analysis of family board games, shared failure creates deeper strategy because the group must work together to overcome the game’s systems. When the main course is cooperative or highly interactive, the winner is not the person with the best strategy, but the person who facilitated the best conversation.

Course Three: The Light Dessert

By the time you reach the third game, mental fatigue is setting in. Players are tired of making complex decisions, and the room is likely getting warmer. This is where you serve the dessert: a fast, light, and often silly game that requires zero brainpower but maximum laughter.

This is the domain of party games. Games like Dixit are perfect because they reward ambiguity and creativity rather than logic. Dixit players treat the art as a theme. It is actually the mechanic. The game forces players to think about what others are thinking, which keeps the social dynamic active without the stress of competition. Another excellent option is Telestrations, which is essentially Pictionary mixed with telephone. It is fast, it is hilarious, and it guarantees that everyone is laughing by the time the final score is tallied.

Do not underestimate the power of a dessert game. It leaves your guests on a high note. If you end the night with a heavy strategy game, the last impression will be one of frustration. If you end with a silly party game, the last impression is one of fun. That is the memory they will carry home.

Managing the Flow

The host’s role is to be a DJ, not a referee. You must monitor the energy of the room and be willing to cut a game short if it is dragging. If a game is taking twice as long as expected, do not be afraid to declare a winner early and move on. The goal is variety, not completion.

You must also manage the “alpha gamer” problem. In a dinner party, the person who dominates the conversation can easily crush the mood. Use your game selection to mitigate this. Cooperative games naturally suppress alpha gamers because they cannot win alone. Social deduction games naturally elevate quiet players because their lies are just as valuable as anyone else’s. By curating your menu carefully, you can ensure that every guest feels heard and valued.

Finally, remember that the games are secondary to the company. If the conversation is flowing, let it flow. If a game is boring, drop it. The best board game dinner parties are not defined by the games played, but by the connections made.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many games should I plan for a dinner party?

Plan for three games. One warm-up (under 45 minutes), one main course (60-90 minutes), and one dessert (30 minutes). This provides a complete arc without exhausting your guests.

What if my group has mixed experience levels?

Lean heavily on cooperative games for the main course. Games like Forbidden Island or Castle Panic allow new players to contribute meaningfully without being overwhelmed by complex rules. Avoid games with hidden information or asymmetric powers until the group is comfortable.

Can I play a heavy strategy game at a dinner party?

Yes, but only as the main course, and only if your group specifically requests it. Heavy games like Brass: Birmingham or Ark Nova require deep focus and can kill the social vibe if the group is not fully committed. If you must play one, keep it to 120 minutes maximum and be prepared to cut it short.

How do I handle a guest who is losing badly and getting upset?

Use your dessert game to reset the mood. If a player is bitter after the main course, switch to a party game where luck and silliness dominate. This gives them a chance to win on their own terms and re-engage with the group. If the bitterness persists, it is a sign that this guest is not a good fit for future dinner parties.

What is the most important rule for hosting a board game dinner party?

The most important rule is that the host’s job is to facilitate fun, not to win. Your goal is to create a memorable experience for your guests. If that means sacrificing a perfectly played game for a more social one, do it. The games will be forgotten, but the feeling of a great night with friends will last forever.

This is the moment most new players quit Carcassonne. They do not lose because they are bad at games. They lose because they do not understand that Carcassonne is not a game about building things. It is a game about owning things.

The tile-placement trap is the single most common failure mode in Carcassonne, and it has a specific name among veteran players: the orphaned follower. It happens when a player places a tile that connects to an existing feature, but does not place a meeple on that feature first. The feature grows, the points multiply, and the original owner is left watching their opponent cash in on their own construction. The solution is not to play more carefully. The solution is to play aggressively.

Understanding this dynamic requires a fundamental shift in how you view the board. You must stop seeing the board as a canvas for your creativity and start seeing it as a series of contested property lines. Every tile you place is either a deed to your own land, or a gift to your opponent. The game rewards the player who claims ownership early, and it punishes the player who assumes the board will stay static.

Why Your First City Is Not Your City

When you place a city tile, you are creating a potential asset. When you place a meeple on it, you are securing ownership. When you place a tile that connects to that city, you are increasing its value. The trap occurs when you place a tile that connects to a city you own, but you do not place a meeple on the new segment. You are essentially handing your opponent a free expansion of your own property.

Consider a simple scenario. You place a city tile in the center of the board. You put a meeple on the bottom-left corner. You feel smart. You have a city. You will score points when the game ends. Two turns later, your opponent places a tile that connects to the right side of your city. They do not place a meeple. They are building a road that will eventually connect to a monastery. You look at your city, and you realize it is now four tiles wide. You still have one meeple. Your opponent has one meeple on the road. The city is now worth 14 points instead of 8. But you only get 8 points. Your opponent gets 14 points for the road, and you get nothing for the city until the game ends, and even then, you only get 8 points because you only have one meeple.

This is the orphaned follower trap. You built the asset. They claimed the expansion. The math is brutal, and it happens in almost every first game of Carcassonne. The solution is simple: if you want to claim the points, you must place a meeple on the feature before it grows. If you cannot place a meeple, do not place a tile that connects to it. This is the core strategic principle of Carcassonne, and it is the reason why new players consistently lose to experienced ones.

When to Claim and When to Block

Not every feature needs a meeple. Some features are too small to be worth the investment. A single tile city is worth 2 points. A single tile road is worth 1 point. A single tile monastery is worth 8 points, but it takes 8 turns to complete. The decision to place a meeple is a resource management decision. You have a limited number of meeples, and you must decide which features are worth claiming.

The general rule is to claim any feature that can score 10 points or more. This includes cities that are three tiles or larger, roads that are four tiles or larger, and monasteries that are one tile away from completion. Anything smaller is usually not worth the investment, unless you are blocking an opponent from claiming it. This is the second part of the aggressive play strategy: you do not always need to claim a feature to benefit from it. Sometimes, you just need to prevent your opponent from claiming it.

Blocking is a subtle art. It requires you to place a tile that connects to an opponent’s feature, but does not give them a free expansion. You place a tile that extends their road by one tile, but you do not place a meeple. You are essentially saying, “I will not let you finish this road, and I will not let you score it.” This is a powerful defensive move, and it is often overlooked by new players who are too focused on building their own cities.

If you do not place it, they cannot score 4 points until they find another tile. By placing the tile, you are giving them 4 points. The difference is 4 points. In a close game, 4 points is the difference between winning and losing.

The Monastery Mistake

Monasteries are the most misunderstood feature in Carcassonne. They are worth 8 points when completed, but they take 8 turns to complete. New players often ignore them, assuming they are too slow to be worth the investment. This is a fatal error. A completed monastery is worth 8 points, plus 1 point for every city or road that touches it. A well-placed monastery can score 15 points or more, and it is often the deciding factor in a close game.

The key to playing monasteries is timing. You must place a meeple on a monastery when it is one tile away from completion. If you wait until it is completed, you will have missed your chance to claim the points. If you place a meeple too early, you risk having it captured by an opponent before it is completed. The sweet spot is to place a meeple when the monastery has 7 tiles surrounding it. This gives you the maximum possible points, and it minimizes the risk of capture.

You score 8 points, plus 1 point for each of the 7 surrounding tiles. You score 15 points. This is a massive swing, and it is often enough to win the game. But you must be careful. If you place a meeple on a monastery too early, your opponent can place a tile that connects to the monastery, and they will capture your meeple. You will lose your 8 points, and they will gain 8 points. This is the monastery mistake, and it is the most common way experienced players lose to new players who understand the value of monasteries.

How to Play Aggressively Without Being Aggressive

Aggressive play in Carcassonne does not mean placing tiles randomly. It means placing tiles with the specific intent of claiming or blocking features. It means understanding the value of every tile, and using that value to your advantage. It means playing the board, not the tiles.

The first step is to always look at the board before you place a tile. Ask yourself: what features are available? Which ones are worth claiming? Which ones are worth blocking? This simple habit will save you from the orphaned follower trap, and it will help you win more games. The second step is to place a meeple on every feature that can score 10 points or more. The third step is to block your opponent’s features when you cannot claim them yourself. Aggressive play is not always the right strategy. There are times when you should play defensively, and there are times when you should play passively. The key is to read the board, and to adjust your strategy accordingly. If your opponent is playing aggressively, you should play defensively. If the board is open, you should play aggressively.

You are denying them 14 points, and you are forcing them to find another way to score. This is defensive play, and it is often the best strategy when your opponent is playing aggressively.

The endgame of Carcassonne is not about placing more tiles. It is about calculating points. You must know how many points you have, and how many points your opponent has. You must know which features are scoreable, and which ones are not. You must know when to place a tile, and when to pass.

]]> Most players treat the ‘Scoop’ mechanic in Pandemic Legacy: Season 1 as a failure state. They see the word ‘Scoop’ and immediately reach for the red ‘You Lost’ card, assuming the designers made a mistake by including a rule that lets you admit defeat before the game ends. This assumption ignores the actual design intent. The scoop rule is not a bug. It is a deliberate pacing mechanism that protects the campaign’s narrative arc and prevents the game from collapsing into a repetitive grind.

When you understand the scoop rule as a feature, you stop playing to avoid it and start playing to manage it. The difference changes how you approach every single session. This article explains why the designers included the scoop rule, how it functions mechanically, and how to use it to preserve the campaign’s integrity.

What the Scoop Rule Actually Does

Pandemic Legacy: Season 1 introduces a permanent board state that changes after every session. Stickers go on the board. Cards are removed. New rules appear. The game is designed to evolve, but evolution requires a controlled environment. If the game runs too long, the board becomes unreadable. If the game runs too short, the campaign loses its emotional weight. The scoop rule sits exactly in the middle.

According to the official rulebook for Pandemic Legacy: Season 1 (Z-Man Games, 2015), the scoop rule triggers when a player decides they cannot win the current session. They do not lose. They place a ‘Scoop’ token on the board. The game ends immediately. The campaign continues to the next session, but the board state is reset to a ‘Scoop’ state, which imposes a permanent penalty on future games. Usually, this penalty is a permanent reduction in the number of actions available per turn, or the permanent removal of a specific card from the game.

This is not a ‘game over’ screen. It is a ‘pause’ button that carries a cost. The designers could have simply ended the game when you lost. Instead, they gave you the option to end it early, at a price. That price is the feature.

Why the Designers Included the Scoop Rule

Legacy games face a unique problem: time management. A standard game of Pandemic takes 45 to 90 minutes. A legacy campaign requires 12 to 24 sessions. If every session runs to its natural conclusion, even when losing, a campaign can stretch to 30 hours of play. That is not a game. That is a part-time job. The scoop rule exists to cap session length and protect the players from burnout.

When a session is clearly unwinnable, continuing to play is often frustrating. You are making moves that do not matter. You are burning time that could be spent on a fresh session. The scoop rule acknowledges this reality. It allows the group to cut their losses and move forward without discarding the campaign.

The designers also used the scoop rule to teach risk assessment. In standard Pandemic, you play until you lose. In Legacy, you play until you either win, lose, or scoop. This changes the decision-making process. You must constantly evaluate: ‘Is this session worth continuing, or should I take the penalty and save our time?’ This is a higher-level strategic question than ‘How do I win this turn?’ It forces players to think about the campaign, not just the board.

How to Use the Scoop Rule Strategically

Most players wait until they are completely out of options before scooping. This is a mistake. The scoop rule is most effective when used proactively. If you look at the board and realize that the disease outbreaks cannot be contained, or that the event cards are insufficient to handle the remaining threats, scoop early. Do not wait for the inevitable loss. Scoop when you realize the session is lost, not when it is over.

Timing matters. Scooping in the first half of a session is less painful than scooping in the final hour. The penalty is the same, but the time invested is different. This is where the game creates its first major strategic fork: do you play a card that is easy to identify but hard to find (a literal card with a clever clue), or do you play a card that is hard to identify but easy to find (an abstract card with a literal clue)?

The third layer is personal. This is the most dangerous layer, and the one that most groups miss entirely. A card might depict a childhood home, a specific breed of dog, or a strange, obscure landscape. The storyteller plays it because it reminds them of their grandmother’s house. The clue is “Home.” The other players have no idea what the storyteller is thinking. They are left to guess between a literal house, an abstract feeling of nostalgia, or a personal memory. This layer is invisible to the other players. It is a hidden variable that the storyteller alone controls. When you use a personal layer, you are betting that your clue is clear enough to be understood, but vague enough to be untraceable. It is a high-risk, high-reward maneuver that defines the top tier of Dixit play.

The Voting Math: Why Balance Beats Accuracy

The scoring system in Dixit is not a simple accuracy test. It is a complex balancing act that rewards strategic ambiguity. If you are the storyteller, you score points based on how many players vote for your card. If exactly one person votes for it, you score 4 points. If two or more vote, you score 2 points per voter. If no one votes, you score 0. This creates a powerful incentive to be neither too obvious nor too obscure. A clue that is too easy (“The red bicycle”) might get three votes, giving you 6 points. A clue that is too hard (“My third-grade teacher’s middle name”) gets zero votes, giving you 0. The optimal clue is one that lands in the “Goldilocks zone”, hard enough to confuse the literal thinkers, but clear enough to attract a few smart voters.

For the voters, the math is even more punishing. If you vote for the correct card, you score 6 points. If you vote for an incorrect card, you score 0. This seems straightforward, but it creates a massive strategic dilemma. If you vote for the correct card, you reveal your hand to the other players. They now know which card you held, and they know that your clue matched it. This gives them information for the next round. If you vote for an incorrect card, you score 0, but you keep your hand hidden. You also mislead the storyteller, who might think their clue was misunderstood, or they might realize that their clue was too obscure and adjust for the next round.

This is where the game shifts from a creative exercise to a game of information warfare. The best voters are not the ones who find the correct card every time. They are the ones who know when to vote correctly and when to vote incorrectly. Voting incorrectly is a form of denial. It protects your point card for a future round where you can use it as a storyteller, or it confuses the storyteller into making a weaker clue next time. It is a defensive play that sacrifices immediate points for long-term strategic advantage. Most groups play Dixit as a game of “find the card.” The best groups play it as a game of “manage the information flow.”

The Storyteller’s Dilemma: Clarity vs. Obscurity

As the storyteller, you are tasked with writing a clue that is a single word or short phrase. This constraint is the source of all the game’s tension. A long sentence allows you to be specific. A single word forces you to be ambiguous. The art is designed to support this ambiguity. The surreal, dreamlike quality of the illustrations means that almost any card can be interpreted in multiple ways. A card showing a man flying a kite might be about “Freedom,” “Play,” “Height,” or “Strings.” The storyteller must choose one of these angles and commit to it.

The mistake most storytellers make is trying to be clever. They choose a clue that is technically accurate but socially irrelevant. They say “Blue” for a card with a blue sky, or “Bird” for a card with a bird. This is a low-effort clue that attracts low-effort votes. It is also a clue that gives away your point card to anyone who is paying attention. The better storyteller understands that their goal is not to be right. Their goal is to be misunderstood by the right people. They craft a clue that is emotionally resonant but intellectually slippery. They say “Escape” for the kite card, knowing that the literal thinkers will look for a bird, the abstract thinkers will look for freedom, and the personal thinkers might look for a memory of flying kites. They are casting a wide net, knowing that only a few fish will bite, but those fish will be the ones who understand their vision.

This requires a deep understanding of your specific group. Some groups are literal. Some groups are abstract. Some groups are personal. A storyteller who knows their group can tailor their clues to exploit these tendencies. If your group is literal, play abstract cards with literal clues. If your group is abstract, play literal cards with abstract clues. This is the core strategic loop of Dixit: it is a mirror that reflects the cognitive style of your players back at them. The art is the mechanism that allows this reflection to happen.

Why the Art Is the Mechanic

Most board games use art as a thematic overlay. The art on a Catan board is a landscape. The art on a Wingspan card is a bird. The art is there to make the game look nice, but it does not change how the game plays. In Dixit, the art is the game. The surreal, open-ended nature of the illustrations is what makes the game possible. If the art were realistic, the game would collapse into a vocabulary test. If the art were abstract, the game would become too obscure for most players. The specific style of the art, the whimsical, slightly unsettling, highly detailed watercolors, is carefully calibrated to sit in the sweet spot between literal and abstract.

This is why Dixit is often described as a “party game” that plays like a strategy game. It is accessible to children and non-gamers because the art is beautiful and engaging. It is deep for experienced gamers because the scoring math and information management create a complex strategic layer. The art is the bridge between these two audiences. It allows the game to scale in complexity based on the players’ ability to navigate the three layers of meaning. When you understand that the art is the mechanic, you stop looking for the “right” answer and start looking for the “best” answer. You stop trying to win the round and start trying to control the flow of information. You stop playing a game about cards and start playing a game about minds.

The next time you sit down to play Dixit, look past the pretty pictures. Look at the voting math. Look at the information asymmetry. Look at the three layers of meaning. The art is not just there to look at. It is there to be used. It is the engine of the game, and it is the key to winning it.

What Was the Contract Before TableTop?

To understand the magnitude of the shift, you have to look at what crowdfunding looked like before 2012. When board game designers started using it, the model was simple: you raise money to manufacture a prototype. The contract was explicit. Backers were funding the development process, not the final product.

Delays were the norm. A campaign that took 18 to 24 months to deliver was considered a success. Components were often simplified to stay within budget.
